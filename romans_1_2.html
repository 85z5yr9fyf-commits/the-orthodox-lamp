No book of the New Testament has been argued over more than St Paul's letter to the Romans. Augustine was converted reading a verse from it (Romans 13:13–14). Martin Luther said that Romans 1:17 opened the gates of paradise to him, and the Reformation was fought largely over its third and fourth chapters. John Calvin's doctrine of predestination leans heavily on its ninth chapter. For many Protestants, Romans is the clearest statement of the gospel in Scripture.

The Orthodox Church reads Romans too, and has read it for far longer. The Fathers preached on it, wrote commentaries on it, and quoted it constantly. The fullest of these commentaries to survive in its original Greek is the series of thirty-two homilies preached by St John Chrysostom at Antioch. St Paul is read at every Divine Liturgy of the Coptic Church, in the reading called the Pauline, before the Catholic Epistle, the Acts and the Gospel.

The aim of these pages is simple: to let the Fathers explain Romans in their own words, to set beside them the main Protestant readings fairly and in their own words, and to show where the two really differ and where they are closer than people assume.

This first page covers chapters 1 and 2. Paul introduces the gospel, then shows that the whole world stands in need of it: the Gentiles who knew God from creation and turned to idols, and the Jews who had the law and did not keep it. Along the way it raises questions that still divide Christians. What can people know of God without the Bible? Does God cause people to sin when He "gives them up"? And if we are saved by faith, why does Paul say that God "will render to each one according to his deeds"?

"...the well-ordered harmony of all things spoke out more clearly than a trumpet." St John Chrysostom, Homily 3 on Romans

Chrysostom's homilies follow J. B. Morris's translation as revised by W. H. Simcox, in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, First Series, volume 11. Where a work has no public domain English translation, its teaching is summarised and marked PARAPHRASE. Scripture follows the NKJV.

Gospel
εὐαγγέλιον · euangelion
"Good news." For Paul, the announcement of what God has done in Jesus Christ, His Son, "the power of God to salvation for everyone who believes" (1:16).
Righteousness of God
δικαιοσύνη θεοῦ · dikaiosynē theou
The phrase at the heart of Romans (1:17). Read as God's own faithfulness and justice, as the righteousness He gives to us, or both. How it is given is where Orthodox and Protestants part.
Faith
πίστις · pistis
Trust, belief and faithfulness. Paul speaks of "the obedience of faith" (1:5), faith that obeys.
Wrath
ὀργή · orgē
God's just opposition to sin. The Fathers insist it is not a passion or a fit of temper in God, but His righteous judgement (2:5).
Without excuse
ἀναπολόγητος · anapologētos
Having no defence. Paul uses it of those who knew God from creation (1:20) and of those who judge others while doing the same things (2:1).
Gave them up
παρέδωκεν · paredōken
"Handed over" (1:24, 26, 28). The Fathers read it as God withdrawing His help from those who refused Him, not as God pushing them into sin.
Conscience
συνείδησις · syneidēsis
The inner witness that accuses or excuses (2:15). It can be trained or darkened, but it is never wholly lost.
Nature
φύσις · physis
What something is made to be. Paul uses it of what is "natural" and "against nature" (1:26–27) and of Gentiles who "by nature" do what the law requires (2:14).
Repentance
μετάνοια · metanoia
A change of mind and life. God's kindness is meant to lead us to it (2:4).
Circumcision of the heart
περιτομὴ καρδίας · peritomē kardias
The inward cutting away of sin promised in Deuteronomy 30:6. Paul says it is the true circumcision (2:29). Colossians 2:11–12 links it with baptism.

Paul wrote Romans from Corinth, most likely around AD 56 to 58, as he was about to take the collection from the Greek churches to Jerusalem (15:25–26). He had never visited Rome. The church there was a mix of Jewish and Gentile believers, and much of the letter is about how the two belong together in one people of God. Many scholars connect this with the expulsion of Jews from Rome under the emperor Claudius around AD 49 (Acts 18:2) and their return a few years later, which would have left Gentile believers leading a church into which Jewish believers were coming back. That reconstruction is likely but not certain.

St John Chrysostom worked out the date the same way, from the collection for Jerusalem, and placed Romans after the letters to the Corinthians. He began his homilies by telling his people why he loved Paul so much:

St John Chrysostom
Argument to the Homilies on Romans
"As I keep hearing the Epistles of the blessed Paul read, and that twice every week, and often three or four times, whenever we are celebrating the memorials of the holy martyrs, gladly do I enjoy the spiritual trumpet, and get roused and warmed with desire at recognizing the voice so dear to me, and seem to fancy him all but present to my sight, and behold him conversing with me."

Three Keys the Fathers Bring

First, Jew and Gentile. Chrysostom reads Romans as a letter about two peoples being made one. Early in his homilies he says Paul is "breaking through the partition between the circumcision and the uncircumcision." Many Protestants since the Reformation have read Romans mainly as an answer to the question "How can I, a sinner, be right with God?" Both questions are really in the letter. The Fathers simply never forget the first.

Second, faith and life together. The Fathers speak of salvation as a gift received by faith, and in the same breath of a life that must be lived, judged and rewarded. They do not feel the tension between Romans 1:17 and Romans 2:6 that later debates created, because for them faith is the beginning of a life of union with God, not a single moment set over against the rest.

Third, the free will of man. The Fathers before Augustine, and the Greek and Oriental Fathers after him, read Romans with the conviction that God made man free and never forces him. Chrysostom says of 2:8 that Paul chose his words "that one may learn that the fall is one of free choice." This conviction matters most on Romans 9, which has its own page in this series.

Then come the two verses that the whole letter unfolds:

Romans 1:16
"For I am not ashamed of the gospel of Christ, for it is the power of God to salvation for everyone who believes, for the Jew first and also for the Greek."
🔵 Orthodox reading: The gospel is not only a message about power. It is itself God's power at work, and it is offered to Jew and Greek alike.
Romans 1:17
"For in it the righteousness of God is revealed from faith to faith; as it is written, 'The just shall live by faith.'"
🔵 Orthodox reading: God's righteousness is revealed and given in the gospel, and it is received by faith from beginning to end. Paul quotes Habakkuk 2:4.
🟣 Protestant reading: This is the verse of the Reformation. Luther came to see "the righteousness of God" not as the standard by which God condemns sinners but as the gift by which He justifies them through faith.

Where They Agree and Where They Part

Both say that righteousness is God's gift, that we cannot earn it by our labours, and that it is received by faith. That is real common ground, and it should be said plainly.

The difference is what the gift is. For Calvin and the Reformed tradition, justification is God's verdict: He forgives the sinner and counts him righteous for Christ's sake. Regeneration and holiness always follow and are never absent, but they are a distinct gift and form no part of the ground on which we are accepted. For the Fathers, the righteousness God gives is not only a verdict but a healing. Chrysostom says in the same homily that the sinner is "not only to be suddenly freed from punishment but to become just." To be justified is to be forgiven and made righteous by sharing in Christ, which the Orthodox call theosis. That is why the Fathers can speak of faith alone at the start of the Christian life and of works at the judgement without any sense of contradiction, as the next sections show. The fuller argument over justification belongs to Romans 3 and 4.

Paul now turns to why the world needs the gospel. He begins with the Gentiles, and with a claim that every Father took seriously: that God can be known, truly if not fully, from the world He made.

Romans 1:19
"because what may be known of God is manifest in them, for God has shown it to them."
🔵 Orthodox reading: The knowledge of God is not something people must climb to by cleverness. God Himself has shown it to them.
Romans 1:20
"For since the creation of the world His invisible attributes are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made, even His eternal power and Godhead, so that they are without excuse,"
🔵 Orthodox reading: Creation is a real revelation of God's power and divinity, plain enough that those who refuse it have no defence.
Romans 1:21
"because, although they knew God, they did not glorify Him as God, nor were thankful, but became futile in their thoughts, and their foolish hearts were darkened."
🔵 Orthodox reading: The fault was not ignorance but ingratitude. They knew God and would not glorify Him or give thanks, and their hearts grew dark as a result.

The Reformed Reading

The Reformed tradition affirms that creation reveals God, but stresses how little fallen people can do with that knowledge:

John Calvin
Institutes of the Christian Religion I.5.14
"In vain for us, therefore, does Creation exhibit so many bright lamps lighted up to show forth the glory of its Author. Though they beam upon us from every quarter, they are altogether insufficient of themselves to lead us into the right path... When Paul says that that which may be known of God is manifested by the creation of the world, he does not mean such a manifestation as may be comprehended by the wit of man (Rom. 1:19); on the contrary, he shows that it has no further effect than to render us inexcusable."
ReformerVERBATIM · BEVERIDGE TRANSLATION (1845)
Westminster Confession (1646)
Westminster Confession of Faith I.1
"Although the light of nature, and the works of creation and providence, do so far manifest the goodness, wisdom, and power of God, as to leave men inexcusable; yet are they not sufficient to give that knowledge of God, and of his will, which is necessary unto salvation..."

The Fathers

Creation is a real and open book about God, given so that people might know Him and rise to Him. Sin darkens the eye of the soul, but it does not put the light out.

Reformed

Creation reveals God truly, but fallen man cannot rightly receive it. In practice it serves to leave him without excuse until God opens his eyes by faith and Scripture.

The two are closer than they first look. Both agree that no one is saved by nature alone and that all are without excuse. Calvin himself grants that creation gives off "some sparks." The difference is one of emphasis, and it matters: Chrysostom insists that God's purpose in creation was not to condemn but to draw people to Himself, and that condemnation came only from their refusal. That fits the Orthodox conviction that God desires all to be saved (1 Timothy 2:4). It is also why the Orthodox can use the arguments from creation found on Evidence for God with a clear conscience: Paul and the Fathers did the same.

Three times Paul says that God "gave them up": to uncleanness (1:24), to vile passions (1:26) and to a debased mind (1:28). Does that mean God drove them into sin?

Romans 1:24
"Therefore God also gave them up to uncleanness, in the lusts of their hearts, to dishonor their bodies among themselves,"
🔵 Orthodox reading: God's "giving up" is His judgement on a refusal that came first. They exchanged the truth for a lie, and He let them have what they chose.
Romans 1:28
"And even as they did not like to retain God in their knowledge, God gave them over to a debased mind, to do those things which are not fitting;"
🔵 Orthodox reading: The pattern is exact: they did not want God in their knowledge, so God let their minds go where they wished.
St John Chrysostom
Homily 3 on Romans (on 1:24)
"...he shows, that even of the perversion of the laws it was ungodliness which was the cause... For as he that has the command in an army, if upon the battle lying heavy upon him he retreat and go away, gives up his soldiers to the enemies not by thrusting them himself, but by stripping them of his own assistance; thus too did God leave those that were not minded to receive what comes from Him..."

ReformerVERBATIM · OWEN TRANSLATION (1849)

To be fair to Calvin, both readings agree on three things: the people in Romans 1 deserved judgement, God's handing over is a just punishment, and the root of sin is in the sinner. The difference is whether God's role is to withdraw (the Fathers) or to withdraw and also actively arrange (Calvin). The Orthodox concern is that the second, pushed far enough, makes God the author of the very sins He punishes. That question, and how God can govern a world full of sin without causing it, is treated on The Problem of Evil, and it returns in force on Romans 9.

Romans 1:26–27

Romans 1:26-27
"For this reason God gave them up to vile passions. For even their women exchanged the natural use for what is against nature. 🔵 Orthodox reading: Paul gives same-sex relations as an example of the "exchange" he has been describing: turning from the pattern of the Creator to what is against it. The Church has always read these verses as moral teaching.

The Fathers, the Orthodox and Catholic Churches, and most Protestant churches read these verses in the same way. Since the late twentieth century some Protestant scholars and denominations have argued that Paul had in mind only exploitative practices of his day, or acts done out of excess by people who were not so inclined. Their case, and the Church's answer, are set out on Sex & Gender. Two things from Romans itself belong here. First, Paul's list goes straight on to envy, deceit, gossip, pride and disobedience to parents (1:29–31), so no reader escapes it. Second, the very next verse turns on anyone who reads the list with satisfaction.

Paul has led his reader through the sins of the pagan world, and a reader who kept the law would have agreed with every word. Then the trap closes:

Romans 2:1
"Therefore you are inexcusable, O man, whoever you are who judge, for in whatever you judge another you condemn yourself; for you who judge practice the same things."
🔵 Orthodox reading: The person who condemns others for what he does himself has signed his own sentence. This is not a ban on calling sin sin. Paul has just done that. It is a warning against hypocrisy.
St John Chrysostom
Homily 5 on Romans (on 2:1)
"You are depriving yourself of defense, whoever you may be; for when you condemn an adulterer, and yourself commit adultery, although no man condemns you, in your judgment upon the guilty person you have also passed sentence against yourself."

St Irenaeus, writing around AD 180, quotes these very verses to prove that man is free and that God's judgement is just:

St Irenaeus of Lyons
Against Heresies IV.37.1
"God therefore has given that which is good, as the apostle tells us in this Epistle, and they who work it shall receive glory and honour, because they have done that which is good when they had it in their power not to do it; but those who do it not shall receive the just judgment of God, because they did not work good when they had it in their power so to do."

This is the seed of what the Fathers will say about Romans 9. If God's patience leads to repentance and the hard heart stores up its own wrath, then God's dealings with us are not fixed in advance without regard to how we respond. The same patience appears in 9:22, where God "endured with much longsuffering the vessels of wrath."

Here is the passage that every reading of Romans has to explain. Paul, who will soon say that a man is justified by faith apart from works of the law (3:28), says this:

Romans 2:6-7
"who 'will render to each one according to his deeds': eternal life to those who by patient continuance in doing good seek for glory, honor, and immortality;"
🔵 Orthodox reading: There will be a real judgement of real lives. Those who keep on doing good, seeking what God gives, will receive eternal life.
🟣 Protestant reading: Most Protestants agree that the judgement looks at works, but as the evidence and fruit of a faith that alone justifies. Many Reformed readers also see 2:7 as stating the law's standard, which no one in fact meets apart from Christ.
Romans 2:13
"for not the hearers of the law are just in the sight of God, but the doers of the law will be justified;"
🔵 Orthodox reading: Hearing the law is not enough. God justifies those who do it.
John 5:28-29
"Do not marvel at this; for the hour is coming in which all who are in the graves will hear His voice and come forth— those who have done good, to the resurrection of life, and those who have done evil, to the resurrection of condemnation."
🔵 Orthodox reading: Christ Himself describes the last judgement in the same terms.

The Fathers

We are justified by grace through faith, not by works we produce on our own. But faith is the start of a new life, and at the judgement God will look at that life. Good works done in Christ are His gifts in us, and they truly count, because He crowns them.

Calvin and the Reformed

God will reward good works, and only those who persevere in them will be saved. But the works are not the ground of acceptance or a merit before God. On 2:13 Paul is stating the law's demand, which no sinner can meet, so another righteousness must be sought.

Notice how much is shared. Calvin agrees that God "will give eternal life to those who, by attention to good works, strive to attain immortality," and Augustine agrees that no good work is ours apart from God's gift. Nobody here teaches that a person can earn heaven on his own; that was the teaching of Pelagius, which the Fathers condemned and the Council of Ephesus in 431 confirmed. The real question is whether works done in God's grace are part of the way we are finally saved (the Fathers) or only its fruit and evidence (the Reformers). The Orthodox call this synergy: God gives everything, and we freely work with what He gives. The case against faith alone is made in full on Sola Fide.

🔵 Orthodox reading: There is a moral law written into human nature. Even those without the law of Moses know right from wrong, and their conscience bears witness.

Real Gentiles before Christ, like Job and Cornelius, who honoured God and lived by the law of nature. God's judgement had no favourites even then.

The Orthodox Church follows Chrysostom's instinct without claiming more than Scripture says. Every human being bears the image of God, and that image, though damaged by sin, is not destroyed (Genesis 9:6; James 3:9). God is at work everywhere, drawing people toward Him, as He drew Cornelius. But no one is saved apart from Christ, and Cornelius, once he had heard, was baptised. Notably, a number of modern Protestant scholars, among them C. E. B. Cranfield and N. T. 🔵 Orthodox reading: What counts before God is the inward reality. The heart is circumcised by the Spirit, and the praise comes from God, not men.

Protestants agree that true circumcision is inward and the work of the Spirit. The Reformed also see a link between circumcision and baptism, and use it to argue for infant baptism. Baptists and many evangelicals see baptism as an outward sign of an inward change that has already happened. For the Fathers, baptism is where God does the inward work, which is why Paul can say we were circumcised "buried with Him in baptism." This is the theme of Romans 6.

What Every Coptic Christian Should Know

"Come to Me, all you who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest." Matthew 11:28

Home›Oriental Orthodoxy›What Every Coptic Christian Should Know
The Hanging ChurchCoptic Orthodox · Cairo

This page is for two kinds of people. Some of you are new. You may be thinking about joining the Coptic Orthodox Church, or you have just started coming, and everything feels unfamiliar. Others grew up in the Church. You were baptized as a baby, you know the hymns and the smell of the incense, but nobody ever really sat down and explained what it all means, or you drifted away and are thinking about coming back.

Either way, you are welcome. You do not need to know anything to read this page. It explains the basics in plain words: who God is, who Jesus is, what being a Copt means, and how a Coptic Christian lives day to day. At the end there is a simple plan for your first week.

One thing before we start. Christianity is not mainly a list of rules or a set of facts to memorise. It is a relationship with a Person, Jesus Christ, lived inside a family, His Church. Everything on this page is about getting to know Him.
Matthew 11:28
"Come to Me, all you who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest."

All Bible quotations on this page are from the New King James Version. Where the page describes Church practice, it gives the usual practice. Your priest may give you a rule suited to you, and his guidance comes first.

Christians believe in one God. But the one God is not alone or lonely. He has always been a communion of love: the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. We call this the Holy Trinity.

  • The Father is the source. Jesus taught us to call Him "Our Father".
  • The Son is the Word of God, born of the Father before time began. He is the one who became a man: Jesus Christ.
  • The Holy Spirit is the Lord and Giver of life. He comes to live in us at our baptism and chrismation.

These are not three gods, and not one God wearing three masks. They are three Persons who share one divine life. No picture fully explains this, and that is fine. We are not trying to solve God like a puzzle. We are getting to know Him.

Matthew 28:19
"Go therefore and make disciples of all the nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit,"

The Creed

The whole faith is summed up in the Creed, which we say together at every Liturgy. It was written by the councils of Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381). If you learn one thing by heart, learn this. Here it is as the Coptic Church prays it in English:

Light of Light; true God of true God; begotten, not created; of one essence with the Father; by Whom all things were made. Jesus Christ is God the Son, who became a real human being for our sake. He was born of the Virgin Mary, lived among us, taught, healed the sick, forgave sinners, died on the cross, and rose from the dead on the third day. He then ascended to heaven, and He will come again.

John 1:14
"And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we beheld His glory, the glory as of the only begotten of the Father, full of grace and truth."

Why did He come? Because we were lost. Sin cut us off from God, who is the source of life, and so death came into the world. God did not leave us there. The Son became one of us so that He could carry us back. On the cross He gave His life for us. In His resurrection He broke the power of death. Now He shares His life with everyone who is joined to Him.

John 3:16
"For God so loved the world that He gave His only begotten Son, that whoever believes in Him should not perish but have everlasting life."

God and man, one Christ. The Coptic Church, following St Cyril of Alexandria, says that Christ is fully God and fully man, united in one, with nothing mixed up, nothing lost and nothing split apart. You may hear the phrase "one nature of God the Word incarnate". It simply means that the baby in Mary's arms and the man on the cross is the eternal Son of God Himself, not a man who had God living inside him. That is why we can call Mary the Mother of God.

The word Copt simply means Egyptian. It comes from the Greek word for Egyptian, which became qibt in Arabic. So the Coptic Orthodox Church is the ancient Church of Egypt.

  • Founded by St Mark. Church tradition says St Mark, who wrote the second Gospel, brought the faith to Alexandria in the first century. Every Coptic Pope is his successor. Today that is Pope Tawadros II, the 118th Pope of Alexandria, who was enthroned in 2012.
  • Blessed by the Holy Family. When Herod wanted to kill the baby Jesus, Joseph and Mary fled with Him to Egypt (Matthew 2:13–15). Copts treasure the places the Holy Family is remembered to have visited.
  • A Church of martyrs. The Coptic calendar counts its years from AD 284, when the emperor Diocletian came to power and a terrible persecution began. It is called the Year of the Martyrs. Copts have been martyred for Christ in every century since, including our own.
  • A Church of monks. Christian monasticism began in the Egyptian desert with St Antony the Great and St Pachomius. Monasteries are still the beating heart of the Church.
  • Part of a family. The Coptic Church is one of the Oriental Orthodox Churches, together with the Armenian, Syriac, Ethiopian, Eritrean and Indian (Malankara) Churches. They share one faith and one Communion.
Isaiah 19:25
"whom the LORD of hosts shall bless, saying, “Blessed is Egypt My people, and Assyria the work of My hands, and Israel My inheritance.”"
Copts have long seen this prophecy, and the "altar to the LORD in the midst of the land of Egypt" in verse 19, as fulfilled in the Church of Egypt.

You do not have to be Egyptian. The Coptic Church is now spread across the world, in Australia, Europe, the Americas and Africa, and people of every background have joined it. Being a Copt in the full sense means belonging to this Church and sharing its faith, not having a particular family tree.

Salvation is God's gift. We cannot earn it, buy it or deserve it. Christ did what we could never do.

Ephesians 2:8–10
"For by grace you have been saved through faith, and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God, not of works, lest anyone should boast. For we are His workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand that we should walk in them."

But a gift has to be received, and then lived. In the Coptic Church, salvation is not a single moment where you say a prayer and you are done. It is a journey with God for your whole life:

  • We begin with faith and baptism, where we are joined to Christ and receive the Holy Spirit.
  • We grow by prayer, by receiving Holy Communion, by repenting when we fall, and by loving God and our neighbour.
  • We are heading towards becoming like Christ, filled with His life. Scripture calls this becoming "partakers of the divine nature" (2 Peter 1:4).

God does the saving. We say yes to Him, again and again, with our whole lives. When we fall, and we all do, we get up and come back through repentance. That is the Christian life.

Acts 2:38
"Then Peter said to them, “Repent, and let every one of you be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins; and you shall receive the gift of the Holy Spirit."

A sacrament (the Church also calls them the Holy Mysteries) is a prayer of the Church in which God really gives His grace through something we can see and touch: water, oil, bread and wine, the laying on of hands. The Coptic Church has seven.

1. Baptism

The new birth. The person is immersed three times in water, in the name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. Their sins are washed away and they become a child of God.

2. Chrismation

Straight after baptism, the priest anoints the body with holy oil called the Myron. This is the gift of the Holy Spirit, who comes to live in us.

3. Holy Communion

In the Liturgy the bread and wine become the true Body and Blood of Christ. This is the centre of our whole life as Christians (John 6:54).

4. Confession

Also called Repentance. We confess our sins to God in front of a priest, who prays over us and gives us God's forgiveness (1 John 1:9).

5. Unction of the Sick

Prayers and anointing with oil for those who are sick in body or soul, as St James describes (James 5:14–15).

6. Marriage

A man and a woman are joined by God for life. In the Coptic service they are crowned, as a sign that their home is a small kingdom for Christ.

7. Priesthood

The ordination of deacons, priests and bishops, who serve the Church in an unbroken line going back to the apostles.

The main service of the Church is the Divine Liturgy, sometimes called the Holy Mass, or in Arabic the Quddas. It is where we hear God's word and receive Holy Communion. The early Christians did the same.

Acts 2:42
"And they continued steadfastly in the apostles’ doctrine and fellowship, in the breaking of bread, and in prayers."

What happens

  • The prayers and incense. The service usually begins with the Raising of Incense and prayers from the Agpeya.
  • The readings. Readings from St Paul's letters, the other New Testament letters, the Book of Acts, the life of a saint from the Synaxarium, the Psalms and the Gospel. Then usually a sermon.
  • The offering. The bread and wine are offered on the altar, and the priest prays the great prayer in which they become the Body and Blood of Christ.
  • Communion. Those who are prepared come forward to receive.

The Coptic Church uses three Liturgies, named after the saints who handed them down: St Basil, used on most Sundays; St Gregory, often used on great feasts; and St Cyril, used less often. A Sunday Liturgy is long, often two hours or more. That is normal. You are not expected to follow every word at first. Stand when others stand, sit when they sit, and let the prayers wash over you.

Preparing for Holy Communion

Holy Communion is for baptized and chrismated Orthodox Christians who have prepared. The usual preparation is:

  • Go to confession regularly with your father of confession, and do not come to Communion holding on to a serious sin you have not confessed.
  • Fast before Communion. Copts traditionally take no food or drink for nine hours beforehand. If you are sick, pregnant or on medication, ask your priest.
  • Come on time and pray the Liturgy with the congregation, rather than walking in just for Communion.
  • Make peace with anyone you have hurt or who has hurt you, as far as you can.

If you are not yet Orthodox, you are still welcome at every service. Simply do not go up for Communion yet. At the end, everyone may receive the blessed bread (the eulogia) the priest hands out.

Prayer is simply talking with God and listening to Him. You do not need special words. But the Church gives us words to start with, and they are a great gift on the days when we do not know what to say.

The Agpeya

The Agpeya is the Coptic book of prayers for the hours of the day. It has seven main prayer times, each made of psalms, a Gospel reading and short prayers, and each remembering a moment in Christ's life:

  • Prime (morning): Christ's resurrection, and the start of the day with Him.
  • Terce (9 am): the coming of the Holy Spirit.
  • Sext (noon): Christ on the cross.
  • None (3 pm): Christ's death.
  • Vespers (sunset): Christ taken down from the cross.
  • Compline (bedtime): Christ's burial, and getting ready for sleep.
  • Midnight: waiting for Christ's second coming.
Psalm 119:164
"Seven times a day I praise You, Because of Your righteous judgments."

Nobody starts with all seven. A good beginning is the Prime prayer when you wake up and the Compline prayer before bed. Add the Lord's Prayer ("Our Father") through the day, make the sign of the cross before meals, and talk to God in your own words. Short and every day is far better than long and once a month.

A few short prayers to know

  • The Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:9–13), which opens every hour of the Agpeya.
  • "Lord have mercy" (in Coptic and Greek, Kyrie eleison), which you will hear many times in church.
  • The Jesus Prayer: "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner", which many people repeat quietly through the day.

The Bible is God's word, written by people inspired by the Holy Spirit and handed down by the Church. It is not a book to sit on a shelf.

Psalm 119:105
"Your word is a lamp to my feet And a light to my path."
  • Start with a Gospel. The Gospel of St Mark is short, fast and was written by the founder of our Church. Then read the others.
  • Read a little every day, even one chapter, slowly. Ask: what does this show me about Jesus? What is He asking of me?
  • Read with the Church. Each day has its own readings in the Church's lectionary, called the Katameros. Reading the same passages as the whole Church that day is a lovely habit.
  • Ask questions. If something confuses you, bring it to your priest or a Sunday School servant. The Church has read these books for two thousand years, and you are not the first to wonder.

Copts fast more than almost any Christians in the world, over two hundred days a year. Fasting is not a diet and it does not earn God's love. It trains the body to say no to itself, so the soul can say yes to God. It always goes together with more prayer and more giving to the poor.

Matthew 6:16–18
"Moreover, when you fast, do not be like the hypocrites, with a sad countenance. For they disfigure their faces that they may appear to men to be fasting. Assuredly, I say to you, they have their reward. But you, when you fast, anoint your head and wash your face, so that you do not appear to men to be fasting, but to your Father who is in the secret place; and your Father who sees in secret will reward you openly."
Notice that Jesus says "when you fast", not "if".

How Copts fast

  • What we eat: during a fast, the food is vegan, with no meat, chicken, eggs, milk or cheese. In some fasts, fish is allowed.
  • When we eat: in the stricter fasts, many people also go without any food or drink until a set time of day.
  • Who is excused: the sick, the pregnant, young children and others with a real need. Ask your father of confession to help you set a rule you can keep, and build up slowly.

The fasts of the year

  • Wednesdays and Fridays, all year, except the joyful fifty days after Easter.
  • The Great Fast (Lent), 55 days before Easter, the most important fast of all.
  • The Fast of Nineveh, 3 days, about two weeks before Lent, remembering the repentance in the book of Jonah.
  • The Nativity Fast, about 43 days before Christmas on 7 January.
  • The Apostles' Fast, from the day after Pentecost until the feast of Sts Peter and Paul on 12 July.
  • St Mary's Fast, 15 days in August, ending on the feast of her Assumption.

Every fast ends in a feast. The Church year walks us through the life of Christ, so that we live it with Him year after year. There are seven major feasts of the Lord and seven minor ones.

Seven Major Feasts

  • The Annunciation
  • The Nativity (Christmas), 7 January
  • Theophany (the Baptism of Christ), 19 January
  • Palm Sunday
  • The Resurrection (Easter)
  • The Ascension, 40 days after Easter
  • Pentecost, 50 days after Easter

Seven Minor Feasts

  • The Circumcision of Christ
  • The Entry into the Temple
  • The Flight of the Holy Family into Egypt, 1 June
  • The Wedding at Cana
  • The Transfiguration
  • Covenant Thursday (the Last Supper)
  • Thomas Sunday, a week after Easter

Easter is the feast of feasts. Every Sunday is a small Easter, which is why we gather on Sunday, the day Christ rose. The Church also keeps many feasts of St Mary, the angels and the saints.

Copts love St Mary very much, and you will see her icon in every church. We call her the Theotokos, which means Mother of God, because the one she gave birth to is God the Son. We honour her as the greatest of the saints, the one who said yes to God with her whole life. We do not worship her. Worship belongs to God alone.

Luke 1:48
"For He has regarded the lowly state of His maidservant; For behold, henceforth all generations will call me blessed."

Why do we ask the saints to pray for us? For the same reason you would ask a friend to pray for you. The saints are not dead. They are alive with Christ, and they love us. When we ask St Mary, St Mark, St Antony or St Mina to pray for us, we are asking members of our own family who are already close to God.

Hebrews 12:1
"Therefore we also, since we are surrounded by so great a cloud of witnesses, let us lay aside every weight, and the sin which so easily ensnares us, and let us run with endurance the race that is set before us,"

Icons are not idols. They are like family photos, and windows into heaven. When we kiss an icon, the honour goes to the person shown, and through them to God.

Every Copt is encouraged to have a father of confession: one priest who knows you, hears your confession regularly, and guides you. You call him Abouna, which means "our father".

Confession can feel scary the first time. It need not be. You are not telling the priest something God does not already know. You are opening the wound so it can heal. The priest is there as a witness and a doctor of souls, and he has heard everything before. What you say stays between you, him and God.

James 5:16
"Confess your trespasses to one another, and pray for one another, that you may be healed. The effective, fervent prayer of a righteous man avails much."
  • Before you go: spend a few minutes honestly thinking back over your life since your last confession. Some people write a short list.
  • When you go: say plainly what you have done wrong. No need to explain or excuse.
  • Afterwards: the priest prays the prayer of absolution over you. He may give you advice or a small spiritual exercise. Then go home in peace. You are forgiven.

If you grew up in the Church and drifted, you are not alone, and you are not too far gone. Maybe church felt like something your parents did. Maybe you were hurt, bored or busy. Maybe you just never understood why any of it mattered. None of that changes the fact that you were baptized into Christ, and He has not stopped waiting for you.

Luke 15:20
"And he arose and came to his father. But when he was still a great way off, his father saw him and had compassion, and ran and fell on his neck and kissed him."
In the parable of the prodigal son, the father does not wait at the door with a lecture. He runs.

The way back is simple, though not always easy: talk to a priest, go to confession, and start coming to the Liturgy again. You do not have to fix your whole life first.

If you are new, the first step is to come and see. Visit a Coptic church, introduce yourself to the priest, and tell him you want to learn. He will guide you through a time of learning and preparation, and then you will be received into the Church. How you are received depends on your background and is decided by the priest and bishop. The page Becoming Orthodox walks you through it.

Here is a simple plan. Do not try to do everything at once. Pick these up and keep them going.

  • Every morning: make the sign of the cross, pray the Lord's Prayer, and if you can, the Prime prayer from the Agpeya.
  • Every night: before sleep, pray the Compline prayer or simply thank God for the day and ask His forgiveness.
  • Every day: read one chapter of the Gospel of St Mark.
  • This Sunday: go to the Liturgy, on time, and stay to the end.
  • This week: speak to a priest. Ask him to be your father of confession, or if you are new, tell him you want to learn about the faith.
  • This Wednesday and Friday: try fasting from one thing, under your priest's guidance, and give what you save to someone in need.
  • Learn one thing by heart: start with the Creed in Section II.
Holiness is built from small things done faithfully every day. God sees every one of them.

We share with all Christians faith in the Holy Trinity and in Jesus Christ as Lord and Saviour. Like Catholics, we have the seven sacraments and bishops in succession from the apostles, but we do not accept the Pope of Rome's authority over the whole Church or later Western teachings. With Protestants we share love for the Bible, but we read it within the Church's tradition, and we keep the sacraments, the saints and the ancient Liturgy.

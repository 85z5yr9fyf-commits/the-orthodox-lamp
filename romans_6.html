Romans 6: Baptism

At the end of Romans 5 Paul said something daring: "where sin abounded, grace abounded much more." The obvious objection follows at once. If more sin means more grace, why not keep sinning? Paul's answer is one of the great baptismal passages of the New Testament. You cannot go on living in sin, he says, because you died to it. When? In baptism, where you were buried with Christ and raised with Him to walk in newness of life.

Romans 6 is therefore where the question of what baptism is becomes unavoidable. Is it the place where God unites us to Christ's death and resurrection, or a sign that this has already happened by faith, or a public testimony of an inward change? Every major Christian tradition has an answer, and the answers differ more here than almost anywhere else.

The Orthodox answer is the Fathers' answer, and it is the oldest. This page sets it out alongside Luther, Calvin, the Westminster Confession and the Baptist confessions, each in their own words. Some Protestants, it turns out, say more about baptism than many of their followers today realise.

"For Baptism is the Cross. What the Cross then, and Burial, is to Christ, that Baptism has been to us..." St John Chrysostom, Homily 10 on Romans

Baptise
βαπτίζω · baptizō
To dip, plunge or immerse. Used of washing as well as of the Christian sacrament.
Buried with
συνθάπτω · synthaptō
To be buried together with. Paul's word for what happens to us in baptism (6:4; Colossians 2:12).
United, planted together
σύμφυτος · symphytos
Grown together, as a graft grows into a tree (6:5). The older English Bible has "planted together."
Old man
ὁ παλαιὸς ἄνθρωπος · ho palaios anthrōpos
The old self, the whole person as ruled by sin, crucified with Christ (6:6).
Freed, justified
δεδικαίωται · dedikaiōtai
"He who has died has been freed from sin" (6:7). The Greek is the verb "justify," showing that for Paul justification includes liberation from sin's power.
Reckon
λογίζομαι · logizomai
The same word as in Romans 4: count it so. Here, count yourselves dead to sin and alive to God (6:11).
Instruments, weapons
ὅπλα · hopla
Tools or arms. Our bodily members can be weapons for sin or for righteousness (6:13).
Holiness, sanctification
ἁγιασμός · hagiasmos
The process and fruit of being made holy (6:19, 22).
Wages
ὀψώνια · opsōnia
A soldier's ration pay. Sin pays its soldiers in death (6:23).
Gift
χάρισμα · charisma
A free gift of grace. Eternal life is not wages but gift (6:23).
Romans 6:1-2
"What shall we say then? Shall we continue in sin that grace may abound? Certainly not! How shall we who died to sin live any longer in it?"
🔵 Orthodox reading: Grace is not permission. The one who has died to sin cannot keep living in it.
St John Chrysostom
Homily 10 on Romans (on 6:1)
"Since then he showed the greatness of the grace by the greatness of the sins it healed, and owing to this it seemed in the eyes of the unthinking to be an encouragement to sin (for if the reason, they would say, why greater grace was shown, was because we had done great sins, let us not give over sinning, that grace may be more displayed still), now that they might not say this or suspect it, see how he turns the objection back again."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

It is worth noticing that Paul had to answer this objection at all. A gospel of salvation by keeping the law would never be accused of encouraging sin. Paul's gospel was accused of it because it really did announce free grace to sinners. The Orthodox, who stress the life of holiness, need to remember this; Protestants, who stress free grace, need to remember Paul's answer. Both are in the same chapter.

Romans 6:3-4
"Or do you not know that as many of us as were baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into His death? Therefore we were buried with Him through baptism into death, that just as Christ was raised from the dead by the glory of the Father, even so we also should walk in newness of life."
🔵 Orthodox reading: Baptism unites us to Christ's death and burial, so that we may share His resurrection life now and walk in it.
Romans 6:5-7
"For if we have been united together in the likeness of His death, certainly we also shall be in the likeness of His resurrection, knowing this, that our old man was crucified with Him, that the body of sin might be done away with, that we should no longer be slaves of sin. For he who has died has been freed from sin."
🔵 Orthodox reading: We have been grown together with Him in a death like His. The old self was crucified so that sin's power over us might be broken.

"What does being baptized into His Death mean? That it is with a view to our dying as He did. For Baptism is the Cross. St John Chrysostom

"For both the one and the other is a death, but not of the same subject; since the one is of the Flesh, that of Christ; the other of sin, which is our own. As then that is real, so is this. But if it be real, then what is of our part again must be contributed."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION
St John Chrysostom
Homily 10 on Romans (on 6:4)
"When then the fornicator becomes chaste, the covetous man merciful, the harsh subdued, even here a resurrection has taken place, the prelude to the other."

Two things stand out in Chrysostom. First, the death in baptism is "real," as real in its own way as Christ's death on the cross. Second, because it is real, "what is of our part again must be contributed." Baptism is God's act, and it calls for a life that matches it. That is the Orthodox view of baptism in two sentences.

The Baptists

"We believe that Christian Baptism is the immersion in water of a believer, into the name of the Father, and Son, and Holy Ghost; to show forth, in a solemn and beautiful emblem, our faith in the crucified, buried, and risen Saviour, with its effect in our death to sin and resurrection to a new life..."
Baptist ConfessionVERBATIM · BAPTIST CONFESSION, SCHAFF

Many Baptist and evangelical readers also argue that the "baptism" of Romans 6:3 is not water baptism at all but the baptism of the Spirit, by which every believer is joined to Christ (1 Corinthians 12:13). On that reading Romans 6 says nothing about the water.

Orthodox

Baptism is where God unites the believer to Christ's death and resurrection, forgives sins, gives new birth and the Holy Spirit (given in chrismation immediately after). It is God's act, received in faith, and it must be lived out.

Lutheran

Baptism works forgiveness and new birth through the Word joined to the water, received by faith. Infants are baptised and regenerated.

Reformed

Baptism is a sign and seal of the covenant, and God really confers the grace it signifies on the elect, though not always at the moment of baptism and not to all who are baptised.

Baptist and many evangelicals

Baptism is an act of obedience and a public emblem of a salvation already received by faith. It is for believers only, by immersion.

Read side by side, the Orthodox view has more in common with Luther, and even with Calvin's comment on Romans 6, than with the purely symbolic view that is now common in evangelical churches. The symbolic view has a harder time with Paul's language. Paul does not say baptism shows that we died with Christ; he says we were buried with Him "through baptism." More on this is on Infant Baptism and Salvation.

From the first generation after the apostles, the Church spoke of baptism as the place of new birth. There is no early Christian writer who treats it as a mere symbol.

Acts 22:16

🔵 Orthodox reading: Ananias to Paul himself: be baptised and wash away your sins.

Notice that Titus 3:5 puts "not by works" and "the washing of regeneration" side by side. For the Fathers there was no tension. Baptism is not a work we do to earn salvation; it is the place where God, in mercy, does the saving work. That is why baptism and "faith alone" were never set against each other in the early Church.

The Coptic Church, like all the Orthodox Churches, baptises by three full immersions in the name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. The Fathers saw in this the three days of Christ in the tomb, and the burial of Romans 6:4 made visible.

St Basil the Great
On the Holy Spirit 15.35
"...the water receiving the body as in a tomb figures death, while the Spirit pours in the quickening power, renewing our souls from the deadness of sin unto their original life. This then is what it is to be born again of water and of the Spirit, the being made dead being effected in the water, while our life is wrought in us through the Spirit. In three immersions, then, and with three invocations, the great mystery of baptism is performed..."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION
St Cyril of Jerusalem
Mystagogical Lecture 2.4
"...and you made that saving confession, and descended three times into the water, and ascended again; here also hinting by a symbol at the three days burial of Christ."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Is immersion required? The earliest church order outside the New Testament, the Didache (late first or early second century), prefers running water and immersion but allows pouring when that is impossible:

The Didache
Didache 7
"...baptize into the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit, in living water. But if you have not living water, baptize into other water; and if you can not in cold, in warm. But if you have not either, pour out water thrice upon the head into the name of Father and Son and Holy Spirit."
Early WitnessVERBATIM · ANF TRANSLATION

The Orthodox keep full immersion as the normal form, because it shows what Paul describes, while recognising that in real necessity, such as grave illness, the Church has always allowed exceptions. Here the Orthodox and the Baptists agree on the form, while disagreeing about who may be baptised and what baptism does. The Westminster Confession, by contrast, says that "dipping of the person into the water is not necessary."

In 6:7 Paul uses the verb "justify": literally, "he who has died has been justified from sin." Every English Bible translates it "freed," because that is what it means here. It is a small but important sign that for Paul justification is not only a verdict but a liberation, a point the Fathers never lost and that was discussed on Romans 3–4.

🔵 Orthodox reading: Because we are under grace, sin no longer rules us. So we must not let it reign, but offer our bodies to God as instruments of righteousness.

"It is this; that our body, before Christ's coming, was an easy prey to the assaults of sin. For after death a great swarm of passions entered also. Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

This is the difference grace makes, in the Fathers' reading. The law told people what to do but gave no power to do it. Grace, given in baptism and by the Spirit, gives the power. "Under grace" does not mean free from God's commandments; it means freed to keep them. The body, which Paul does not despise, becomes an instrument of righteousness, which is one reason the Orthodox fast, bow, make the sign of the cross and pray with the body. See Fasting.

Romans 6:15-18
"What then? Shall we sin because we are not under law but under grace? Certainly not! Do you not know that to whom you present yourselves slaves to obey, you are that one's slaves whom you obey, whether of sin leading to death, or of obedience leading to righteousness? But God be thanked that though you were slaves of sin, yet you obeyed from the heart that form of doctrine to which you were delivered. And having been set free from sin, you became slaves of righteousness."
🔵 Orthodox reading: Everyone serves someone. Set free from sin, we have become slaves of righteousness, by obeying from the heart the teaching handed on to us.
Romans 6:19
"I speak in human terms because of the weakness of your flesh. For just as you presented your members as slaves of uncleanness, and of lawlessness leading to more lawlessness, so now present your members as slaves of righteousness for holiness."
🔵 Orthodox reading: Paul calls for the same energy in holiness that was once given to sin.

Paul says they "obeyed from the heart that form of doctrine to which you were delivered" (6:17). The Greek speaks of a "pattern of teaching" into which they were handed over, the tradition received at baptism. For the Fathers this was the baptismal creed and catechesis, which is why the Church still teaches catechumens before baptism and has them confess the faith at the font. Freedom in Christ is not freedom from all masters, but freedom to serve the right one.

Many Protestants rightly stress that sanctification is the fruit of justification. The Orthodox agree, and would add only that the two are not separate gifts but one life. Paul ends the paragraph: "you have your fruit to holiness, and the end, everlasting life" (6:22). Holiness is the road on which eternal life is reached.

🔵 Orthodox reading: Sin pays wages, and they are death. God pays no wages; He gives eternal life as a gift in Christ.

This verse is a good place for Orthodox and Protestant readers to stand together: no one earns eternal life, and no one who has received it is free to despise it.

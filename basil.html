Etchmiadzin CathedralArmenian Apostolic · Vagharshapat

St Basil, Archbishop of Caesarea in Cappadocia, died on the first day of 379, aged about fifty. In those few years he defended the divinity of the Holy Spirit when even many who accepted Nicaea hesitated, gave the Church the clear distinction between one essence and three hypostases in God, shaped the monastic life of the Christian East, built a whole quarter of his city to care for the sick and the poor, and faced down an emperor. Two years after his death the Council of Constantinople confessed the Holy Spirit "the Lord and Giver-of-Life," who "with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified," in words that echo his.

For the Coptic faithful, Basil is above all a name heard every Sunday. The Divine Liturgy most often prayed in the Coptic Church is the Liturgy of St Basil.

This is the second page in the series on the Oriental pillars. Athanasius, the first, defended the Son against Arius; Basil, who wrote to him as the Church's "physician," carried the same defence over to the Holy Spirit. Cyril of Alexandria is the third.

"...the honour paid to the image passes on to the prototype." St Basil, On the Holy Spirit 18.45

A Note on Quotations

Every quotation marked VERBATIM has been checked word for word against the public domain translation in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, second series, volume 8 (Basil) and volume 7 (Gregory the Theologian). Several of Basil's most famous works, including Against Eunomius, the monastic Rules and the sermons on wealth, have no public domain English translation. Their teaching is summarised and marked PARAPHRASE. Scripture follows the NKJV.

Ousia
οὐσία: "essence"
What God is: the one Godhead common to Father, Son and Holy Spirit.
Hypostasis
ὑπόστασις: "subsistence, person"
Each of the Three in their distinct reality: Father, Son, Holy Spirit. Basil fixed the distinction between ousia and hypostasis for later theology.
Eunomius
Anomoean, d. c. 393
A radical Arian who taught that the Son is "unlike" (anomoios) the Father and that God's essence can be fully known as "unbegotten." Basil wrote three books against him.
Pneumatomachi
"fighters against the Spirit"
Those who accepted that the Son is God but denied that the Holy Spirit is. Basil's On the Holy Spirit answers them.
Doxology
δοξολογία: "word of glory"
The prayer of praise. Basil was attacked for saying "Glory to the Father with the Son together with the Holy Spirit."
Economy
οἰκονομία: "management, wise handling"
Pastoral prudence: Gregory the Theologian uses it of Basil's care in how he spoke of the Spirit in public.
Agrapha
ἄγραφα: "unwritten things"
Teachings and practices received from the apostles through the Church's life rather than in writing.
Hexaemeron
"the six days"
Basil's nine homilies on the creation account in Genesis 1.

"the new city"

Basil was born around 329 or 330 into a family of saints. His grandmother, Macrina the Elder, had treasured the teaching of St Gregory the Wonderworker; his sister Macrina the Younger and his brothers Gregory of Nyssa and Peter of Sebaste are all venerated as saints.

By the 360s the most radical Arians, led by Aetius and Eunomius, taught that God's whole essence is captured in the word "unbegotten." Since the Son is begotten, they said, He is of a different essence: "unlike" the Father. Their claim rested on a bold idea, that the human mind can know exactly what God is.

St Basil
Against Eunomius I–III (c. AD 364)
Basil answers that the names we give God, such as "unbegotten," "good" or "eternal," are true, but none of them defines His essence. We form them from His works, by reasoning about what He has done. "Unbegotten" tells us that the Father has no source, not what the Father is. To claim to know God's essence is not a higher theology but pride. And "Father" and "Son" are names of relation: they show that the Son is from the Father and of the same nature, not that He is unlike Him.

"The operations are various, and the essence simple, but we say that we know our God from His operations, but do not undertake to approach near to His essence. His operations come down to us, but His essence remains beyond our reach. But, it is replied, if you are ignorant of the essence, you are ignorant of Himself. Retort, If you say that you know His essence, you are ignorant of Himself."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION
1 Corinthians 2:10-11
"But God has revealed them to us through His Spirit. For the Spirit searches all things, yes, the deep things of God. For what man knows the things of a man except the spirit of the man which is in him? Even so no one knows the things of God except the Spirit of God."
🔵 Shared reading: Only the Spirit of God knows the things of God. We know God truly, because He reveals Himself; we do not comprehend Him.

Basil's humility before the mystery of God is not vagueness. He is precise about what can be said and honest about what cannot. This is the spirit of every Orthodox liturgy, where God is praised as beyond all understanding and yet truly known.

Athanasius had shown, at the council of 362, that some who said "one hypostasis" and some who said "three hypostases" meant the same faith. Basil gave the Church a settled vocabulary so that the confusion would not return: one ousia, the Godhead the Three share; three hypostases, the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit in their distinct reality.

St Basil
Letter 236, to Amphilochius, 6 (c. AD 376)
"The distinction between οὐσία and ὑπόστασις is the same as that between the general and the particular; as, for instance, between the animal and the particular man. Wherefore, in the case of the Godhead, we confess one essence or substance so as not to give a variant definition of existence, but we confess a particular hypostasis, in order that our conception of Father, Son and Holy Spirit may be without confusion and clear."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Basil's analogy has limits, and he knew it: three men are three separate beings, but the Three in God are one God, never separated in will or action. The analogy shows only how the two words differ. What it secures is that we neither merge the Persons into one, as Sabellius did, nor divide the Godhead into three, as tritheism would.

St Basil
Letter 52, to the Canonicae, 1 (c. AD 370)
"...as being heirs of the same Fathers who once at Nicæa promulgated their great decree concerning the faith. Of this, some portions are universally accepted without cavil, but the homoousion, ill received in certain quarters, is still rejected by some. These objectors we may very properly blame, and yet on the contrary deem them deserving of pardon. To refuse to follow the Fathers, not holding their declaration of more authority than one's own opinion, is conduct worthy of blame, as being brimful of self-sufficiency."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Here are Athanasius' two instincts together: firmness on the faith of Nicaea, and patience with those who struggled over a word because others had misused it. A note for readers of the Christology pages: in the doctrine of the Trinity the Oriental Orthodox use Basil's vocabulary exactly. In speaking of Christ, St Cyril used the word physis, nature, in a related but distinct sense, which is why "one incarnate nature" does not mean what a later reader might assume. See Miaphysite Christology.

Basil's great treatise began with an argument over a single word. At prayer he sometimes said "Glory to the Father through the Son in the Holy Spirit," and sometimes "Glory to the Father with the Son together with the Holy Spirit." Some objected that the second form was new and ranked the Spirit with God.

St Basil
On the Holy Spirit 1.3 (c. AD 375)
"Lately when praying with the people, and using the full doxology to God the Father in both forms, at one time with the Son together with the Holy Ghost, and at another through the Son in the Holy Ghost, I was attacked by some of those present on the ground that I was introducing novel and at the same time mutually contradictory terms."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Matthew 28:19
"Go therefore and make disciples of all the nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit,"
🔵 Orthodox reading: One of Basil's decisive arguments. The Lord Himself joined the Spirit to the Father and the Son in the one Name of baptism. To rank Him with creatures is to break the Name in which we were baptized.
St Basil
On the Holy Spirit 27.68 (c. AD 375)
"They must now instruct us either not to baptize as we have received, or not to believe as we were baptized, or not to ascribe glory as we have believed. Let any man prove if he can that the relation of sequence in these acts is not necessary and unbroken; or let any man deny if he can that innovation here must mean ruin everywhere."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Baptism, faith and praise belong together. We are baptized into the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit; therefore we believe in all three; therefore we glorify all three together. The doxology is not a liturgical accident. It is the creed sung.

The Work of the Spirit

St Basil
On the Holy Spirit 16.38
"And in the creation bethink you first, I pray you, of the original cause of all things that are made, the Father; of the creative cause, the Son; of the perfecting cause, the Spirit; so that the ministering spirits subsist by the will of the Father, are brought into being by the operation of the Son, and perfected by the presence of the Spirit. ... For the first principle of existing things is One, creating through the Son and perfecting through the Spirit."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION
Psalm 33:6
"By the word of the Lord the heavens were made, And all the host of them by the breath of His mouth."
🔵 Orthodox reading: The Word and the Breath (Spirit) of the Lord together make the heavens. Basil reads the Spirit's perfecting work in creation in verses like this.
St Basil
On the Holy Spirit 9.23
"Hence comes foreknowledge of the future, understanding of mysteries, apprehension of what is hidden, distribution of good gifts, the heavenly citizenship, a place in the chorus of angels, joy without end, abiding in God, the being made like to God, and, highest of all, the being made God."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

This is Athanasius' argument carried over to the Spirit. Only God can make us partakers of God. If the Spirit makes us "like to God" and, "highest of all," makes us god by grace, He cannot be a creature. See Theosis & Deification.

Acts 5:3-4
"But Peter said, 'Ananias, why has Satan filled your heart to lie to the Holy Spirit and keep back part of the price of the land for yourself? While it remained, was it not your own? And after it was sold, was it not in your own control? Why have you conceived this thing in your heart? You have not lied to men but to God.'"
🔵 Orthodox reading: To lie to the Holy Spirit is to lie to God. The apostle speaks of the Spirit as God without needing to argue it.

One Worship, Not Three

St Basil
On the Holy Spirit 18.45
"Because we speak of a king, and of the king's image, and not of two kings. The majesty is not cloven in two, nor the glory divided. The sovereignty and authority over us is one, and so the doxology ascribed by us is not plural but one; because the honour paid to the image passes on to the prototype. Now what in the one case the image is by reason of imitation, that in the other case the Son is by nature..."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Basil's sentence about the image and the prototype is about the Son, the perfect image of the Father, and it shows why worshipping the Son is not worshipping a second God. Four centuries later the defenders of icons took the same sentence as the key to their case: honour shown to an icon passes to the one it depicts. See Icon Veneration.

There is a striking fact about Basil. In On the Holy Spirit he gives the Spirit every honour that belongs to God, but he does not, in so many words, write "the Spirit is God." Some of his own friends were troubled by this, and his enemies accused him of heresy or cowardice. Gregory the Theologian wrote to tell him so.

St Gregory the Theologian
Letter 58, to Basil (c. AD 372)
"Many people have condemned us as not firm in our faith; those, I mean, who think and think rightly that we thoroughly agree. Some openly charge us with heresy, others with cowardice; with heresy, those who believe that our language is not sound; with cowardice, they who blame our reserve."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

After Basil's death Gregory explained the reason. Basil was not uncertain. He was guarding his see, and with it the orthodox teaching of a whole region, from an Arian emperor waiting for a pretext to remove him.

St Gregory the Theologian
Oration 43, Funeral Oration on St Basil, 68 (c. AD 381)
"The enemy were on the watch for the unqualified statement the Spirit is God; which, although it is true, they and the wicked patron of their impiety imagined to be impious; so that they might banish him and his power of theological instruction from the city... Accordingly, by the use of other terms, and by statements which unmistakably had the same meaning, and by arguments necessarily leading to this conclusion, he so overpowered his antagonists, that they were left without reply..."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

"Statements which unmistakably had the same meaning." Once again a Father puts meaning above a particular word, as Athanasius did with the "like in essence" party and with the Antiochenes. The Church judged him by what he taught, not by whether he used one formula. Two years after his death, the Creed of 381 also confessed the Spirit's divinity without the bare words "the Spirit is God," by calling Him Lord, Giver of Life, and worshipped and glorified with the Father and the Son.

The Pneumatomachi demanded a Bible verse for the doxology "with the Spirit." Basil answered that much of what all Christians do and believe is received through the Church's life rather than written down, and he gave examples his opponents practised themselves.

St Basil
On the Holy Spirit 27.66 (c. AD 375)
"Of the beliefs and practices whether generally accepted or publicly enjoined which are preserved in the Church some we possess derived from written teaching; others we have received delivered to us in a mystery by the tradition of the apostles; and both of these in relation to true religion have the same force. ... For instance, to take the first and most general example, who is thence who has taught us in writing to sign with the sign of the cross those who have trusted in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ? What writing has taught us to turn to the East at the prayer? Which of the saints has left us in writing the words of the invocation at the displaying of the bread of the Eucharist and the cup of blessing?"
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION
2 Thessalonians 2:15
"Therefore, brethren, stand fast and hold the traditions which you were taught, whether by word or our epistle."
🔵 Orthodox reading: Paul himself names two channels of tradition, "by word" and "by our epistle," and asks for both to be held. Basil's distinction is Paul's.
🟣 Protestant reading: Paul's oral teaching was later written in Scripture, so Scripture alone now carries the apostolic tradition.

Every one of Basil's examples is still part of Coptic worship: the sign of the cross, prayer facing east, and the words of the invocation of the Holy Spirit over the bread and cup. Note also what Basil does not say. He does not set unwritten tradition against Scripture. Both carry "the same force" because both come from the apostles, and he spends most of his treatise proving the Spirit's divinity from Scripture. See Scripture and Tradition.

St Basil
On the Holy Spirit 12.28 (c. AD 375)
"Faith and baptism are two kindred and inseparable ways of salvation: faith is perfected through baptism, baptism is established through faith, and both are completed by the same names. For as we believe in the Father and the Son and the Holy Ghost, so are we also baptized in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Ghost..."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

St Basil
Letter 93, to the Patrician Caesaria, on Communion (c. AD 372)
"It is good and beneficial to communicate every day, and to partake of the holy body and blood of Christ. For He distinctly says, He that eats my flesh and drinks my blood has eternal life. And who doubts that to share frequently in life, is the same thing as to have manifold life. I, indeed, communicate four times a week, on the Lord's day, on Wednesday, on Friday, and on the Sabbath, and on the other days if there is a commemoration of any Saint."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

"Whoever eats My flesh and drinks My blood has eternal life, and I will raise him up at the last day."
🔵 Orthodox reading: Basil quotes this verse as the reason for frequent communion. The Eucharist is "the holy body and blood of Christ," and to receive it is to share in life.
🟣 Protestant reading: Many Protestants read the discourse as a call to believe in Christ, with eating and drinking as images of faith.

The same letter records that in Alexandria and Egypt "each one of the laity, for the most part, keeps the communion, at his own house," a practice from times of persecution and from the desert. Basil writes of it as an accepted custom. The Church later regulated it closely, but his letter is a precious glimpse of how highly the Egyptian Church of his day prized the holy Mysteries. See The Seven Mysteries of Christ.

St Basil

Basil prefers the common life to the life of the solitary. Alone, a man cannot practise the commandments of love: he has no one to serve, no one to correct him, and no one with whom to share the gifts of the Spirit. He asks the hermit, in effect, whose feet he will wash, since the Lord who washed His disciples' feet commanded us to do the same.

Basil did not invent community monasticism; St Pachomius had founded communities in Upper Egypt a generation earlier. What Basil did was to write the principles of the common life with such clarity that his Rules became a foundation for monastic life across the Christian East. The Egyptian desert and the Cappadocian monastery speak with one voice. See Monasticism & the Desert Fathers.

During the famine of 368, before he became archbishop, Basil used his own means and fed the hungry himself.

The emperor Valens was an Arian, and he set out to bring the Church of Cappadocia into line. His prefect Modestus summoned Basil and threatened him.

St Gregory the Theologian
Oration 43, Funeral Oration on St Basil, 49 (c. AD 381)
"What are these? said Basil, pray, inform me. Confiscation, banishment, torture, death. Have you no other threat? said he, for none of these can reach me. How indeed is that? said the prefect. Because, he replied, a man who has nothing, is beyond the reach of confiscation; unless you demand my tattered rags, and the few books, which are my only possessions. Banishment is impossible for me, who am confined by no limit of place... As for tortures, what hold can they have upon one whose body has ceased to be? ... Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

"Amazed at this language, the prefect said, No one has ever yet spoken thus, and with such boldness, to Modestus. Why, perhaps, said Basil, you have not met with a Bishop, or in his defense of such interests he would have used precisely the same language."

Valens himself then came to Caesarea and attended the liturgy at Epiphany. Gregory describes the emperor overwhelmed by the singing of the psalms and the calm of the archbishop at the altar, so shaken that he staggered as he brought his offering (Oration 43, 52). Basil was not exiled. The emperor left him in his see, and Cappadocia remained Nicene.

The Liturgy of St Basil

The Coptic Church prays three anaphoras: those of St Basil, St Gregory and St Cyril. The Liturgy of St Basil is by far the most used, prayed at most Sunday and weekday liturgies throughout the year. Its anaphora gives thanks for creation, the fall, the prophets and the whole saving work of Christ, and calls down the Holy Spirit on the gifts.

How much of this anaphora Basil himself wrote is debated. The Coptic form belongs to an Egyptian family of texts that is shorter and simpler than the Byzantine Liturgy of St Basil, and an early Sahidic Coptic manuscript preserves an older stage of it. There is no clear evidence that Basil wrote the Egyptian form. Many scholars think it is older, and that Basil may have known it, perhaps from his visit to Egypt, and expanded it into the longer Byzantine form. The Coptic Synaxarium honours him as the author of the liturgy that bears his name. Either way, Coptic Christians who pray it are praying a text bound up with Basil's name and with Egypt, the land he visited to learn holiness.

His Feast and His Canons

The Coptic Church remembers his departure on 6 Tobi. The Synaxarium recalls him as the one who "set down canons that believers still follow": his canonical letters on penance and church discipline (Letters 188, 199 and 217) became part of the Church's canon law in the East.

Basil and Athanasius

Basil wrote several letters to Athanasius in the last years of the older bishop's life, seeking his help to heal the divisions of the Eastern churches. They show how the second of the pillars looked to the first.

St Basil
Letter 82, to Athanasius, Bishop of Alexandria (c. AD 372)
"When I turn my gaze upon the world, and perceive the difficulties by which every effort after good is obstructed, like those of a man walking in fetters, I am brought to despair of myself. But then I direct my gaze in the direction of your reverence; I remember that our Lord has appointed you to be physician of the diseases in the Churches; and I recover my spirits, and rise from the depression of despair to the hope of better things."

Armenia and Cappadocia

Caesarea, Basil's see, was where St Gregory the Illuminator was consecrated as the first bishop of the Armenians early in the fourth century, and Armenian bishops long continued to look to Caesarea. Basil is held in great honour in the Armenian Apostolic Church, which has also preserved an anaphora under his name.

The Road to 381

The Oriental Orthodox receive the Council of Constantinople of 381 as the second ecumenical council. Basil died two years before it met, but his brother Gregory of Nyssa and his friend Gregory the Theologian were among its leading figures, and the clauses on the Holy Spirit in the Creed carry his thought. See Understanding the Nicene Creed.

OO Response: Basil gives the Spirit everything that belongs to God: He is ranked with the Father and the Son in baptism, He perfects creation, He makes us god by grace, and He receives the same glory. Gregory the Theologian, who knew Basil better than anyone, explains that Basil held back the bare phrase for a time to protect his church from an Arian emperor, while teaching "by statements which unmistakably had the same meaning" (Section VII). Gregory himself said it plainly, and the Council of 381 confessed it.

The Spirit's deity is also not a fourth-century invention. Peter tells Ananias that lying to the Spirit is lying to God (Acts 5:3-4), and the Lord places the Spirit in the one Name of baptism (Matthew 28:19).

OO Response: Basil's examples are modest and verifiable: the sign of the cross, facing east to pray, the words of the Eucharistic invocation, the blessing of baptismal water. These were practised by every church of his day, including by his opponents. He gives them weight because they are universal and apostolic, not because a bishop invented them.

And Basil does not use tradition to avoid Scripture. Most of On the Holy Spirit is careful exegesis. His point is that Christians have always received the faith through both channels Paul names in 2 Thessalonians 2:15.

OO Response: Basil rejected this charge directly. The three hypostases share one essence, one will, one operation and one glory: "the sovereignty and authority over us is one, and so the doxology ascribed by us is not plural but one" (On the Holy Spirit 18.45). The Father creates through the Son and perfects through the Spirit; "the first principle of existing things is One."

OO Response: Basil used the best language of his education to protect the faith, as Paul used the Greek of his day. But the content of his argument is biblical: the baptismal Name, the work of the Spirit in Scripture, the worship of the Church. It was Eunomius, not Basil, who claimed that the human mind could define God's essence. Basil's answer, that we know God from His works but cannot grasp what He is, is a confession of the limits of philosophy, not a surrender to it.

OO Response: Basil describes the custom of keeping the consecrated gifts at home, which arose in persecution and in the desert, and he is careful to say that the gifts were first offered and given by the priest: "when once the priest has completed the offering, and given it, the recipient... is bound to believe that he properly takes and receives it from the giver." The Eucharist still comes from the Church's priesthood. The letter shows how precious the Mysteries were, not that they could be made without a priest.

What is certain is that it has carried his name in Egypt for many centuries and remains the Coptic Church's main liturgy (Section XIII).

Basil, his friend Gregory of Nazianzus (Gregory the Theologian) and his brother Gregory of Nyssa. Their writings shaped the doctrine of the Trinity confessed at the Council of Constantinople in 381. The Coptic Church prays the Liturgy of St Gregory, attributed to Gregory the Theologian, on major feasts.

The icon controversy came four centuries after him, so he did not write about it directly. But his sentence "the honour paid to the image passes on to the prototype," written about the Son as the image of the Father, became the foundation of the Church's defence of icons. See Icon Veneration.

They never met, as far as we know, but Basil wrote to him several times between 371 and 373, seeking his help for the troubled churches of the East and calling him the "physician of the diseases in the Churches" (Letter 82).

Gregory of Nyssa's Life of Macrina says that when Basil came home proud of his learning, their sister Macrina drew him to the life of prayer and poverty. Basil himself credits his grandmother Macrina the Elder with his first teaching in the faith (Letter 204).

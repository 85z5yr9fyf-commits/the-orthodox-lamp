Mary: Theotokos & Perpetual Virginity

"...henceforth all generations will call me blessed." Luke 1:48

✦ ✦ ✦
Home›Oriental Orthodoxy›Mary the Perpetual Virgin
Parumala ChurchMalankara Orthodox · Kerala

The Oriental Orthodox Churches, Coptic, Ethiopian, Eritrean, Syriac, Armenian and Indian, give the Virgin Mary two titles above all others. She is the Theotokos, the one who gave birth to God. And she is the Aeiparthenos, the Ever-Virgin, who remained a virgin before, during and after the birth of Christ and bore no other child.

Many Protestants accept the first title, at least in principle, and reject the second. They point to the "brothers" of Jesus, to Matthew's "till she had brought forth," and to Luke's "firstborn." This page answers those arguments from Scripture, and it lets the Fathers of the Church speak for themselves on the question that matters most: did Mary have other children? Their answer, given again and again across Egypt, Syria, Asia Minor, Italy and North Africa, is no.

The page is also honest about where the evidence stops. The title Theotokos, as the Council of Ephesus defined it, is a statement about Christ. It does not by definition contain the doctrine of her lasting virginity. What the Fathers show is something different and in its way stronger: the same Church that confessed her the God-bearer confessed her the Ever-Virgin, and several of the Fathers argued directly from the one to the other. Section X sets out how.

"...henceforth all generations will call me blessed." Luke 1:48

A Note on Quotations

Every quotation marked VERBATIM has been checked word for word against a public domain translation, chiefly the Ante-Nicene and Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers. Where no public domain English translation exists, the Father's teaching is summarised and marked PARAPHRASE. Scripture follows the NKJV.

Theotokos
Θεοτόκος: "she who gave birth to God"
Mary's title as the mother of the eternal Son made flesh. Upheld at the Council of Ephesus in 431. It says first of all who Christ is.
Christotokos
Χριστοτόκος: "she who gave birth to Christ"
The title Nestorius preferred, because he would not say that God was born of Mary. Ephesus rejected his teaching.
Aeiparthenos
ἀειπάρθενος: "ever-virgin"
Mary's title as the one who remained a virgin all her life. St. Athanasius already uses it in the fourth century.
Adelphos
ἀδελφός: "brother"
The Greek word used of the "brothers of the Lord." In the Greek Old Testament it translates the Hebrew ach, which covers brothers, half-brothers, nephews and other kinsmen.
Anepsios
ἀνεψιός: "cousin"
The Greek word for cousin, used once in the New Testament (Colossians 4:10).
Heos
ἕως: "until, till"
The word in Matthew 1:25. It marks what was true up to a point, without saying that things changed afterwards.
Prototokos
πρωτότοκος: "firstborn"
The child who first opens the womb, who must be consecrated to God (Exodus 13:2), whether or not other children follow.
Protoevangelium of James
c. AD 150
An early Christian writing about Mary's birth and childhood. Not Scripture, but the earliest surviving source to say that the Lord's brothers were Joseph's children.
Epiphanian view
after St. Epiphanius of Salamis
The brothers of the Lord were Joseph's sons by an earlier marriage. The view held in the Oriental Orthodox Churches.
Hieronymian view
after St. Jerome
The brothers of the Lord were His cousins. The view that became usual in the West.
Helvidian view
after Helvidius, c. 383
The brothers were sons of Mary and Joseph born after Jesus. Rejected by the ancient Church; common among Protestants today.

Theotokos is first a title about Christ. To call Mary the one who gave birth to God is to say that the child she bore was not a man who later became joined to God, but God the Son Himself, who took flesh from her. A mother gives birth to a person, not to a nature, and the person born of Mary is the eternal Word.

The controversy came to a head in 428, when Nestorius, the new Archbishop of Constantinople, objected to the title. He preferred to call her Christotokos, because he would not say that God was born, suffered or died. St. Cyril of Alexandria saw that this divided Christ into two subjects, a Son of God and a son of Mary, and he defended the title as the guard of the one Christ.

St. Cyril of Alexandria
Second Letter to Nestorius (AD 430)
"This was the sentiment of the holy Fathers; therefore they ventured to call the Holy Virgin, the Mother of God, not as if the nature of the Word or his divinity had its beginning from the Holy Virgin, but because of her was born that holy body with a rational soul, to which the Word being personally united is said to be born according to the flesh."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION
St. Cyril of Alexandria
Twelve Anathemas against Nestorius, Anathema 1 (AD 430)
"If anyone will not confess that the Emmanuel is very God, and that therefore the Holy Virgin is the Mother of God (Θεοτόκος), inasmuch as in the flesh she bore the Word of God made flesh [as it is written, The Word was made flesh] let him be anathema."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Cyril's reason is the person of Christ: Mary is the Mother of God because Emmanuel is God. That is why many Protestants today accept the title in principle while rejecting her lasting virginity. The Lutheran Formula of Concord (1577), for example, says she "is both rightly called and in very deed is the Mother of God." The title does not, by its definition, settle whether Mary had other children. That question is settled by the witness of Scripture read in the Church and by the Fathers, set out in Sections IX to XVI.

In Coptic the title is used in its Greek form, Θεοτόκος. In Syriac she is Yoldat Aloho, the one who gave birth to God.

The word Theotokos is not in Scripture, but what it says is.

The Third Ecumenical Council met at Ephesus in 431, with St. Cyril of Alexandria presiding. It deposed Nestorius and received Cyril's Second Letter to Nestorius as the expression of the faith of Nicaea. Cyril's own letters describe the people of Ephesus escorting the bishops with torches after the verdict, which shows how dearly the faithful already held the title.

The Oriental Orthodox Churches receive Ephesus as the third and last of the ecumenical councils, and Cyril is the great teacher of their Christology. Ephesus did not make Mary a source of grace beside Christ, or a co-redeemer. It confessed that the one she bore is the eternal Son of God.

Ephesus did not define her lasting virginity. That faith appears in the same Fathers who fought for the title: St. Athanasius calls her both "Bearer of God" and "Ever-Virgin" (Section VI). Later councils gathered the two titles into one phrase. The Second Council of Constantinople in 553, a council the Oriental Orthodox do not receive, speaks of "the holy, glorious, and ever-virgin Mary, the Mother of God," and the Coptic liturgy greets her in the same way to this day.

Ephesus did not invent the title. It was already in use in Alexandria a century before the council.

St. John of Damascus wrote in the eighth century from within the Chalcedonian Church, and he is not among the Fathers of the Coptic Church. He is quoted here and in Section X because on the Theotokos and the Ever-Virgin he hands on what the whole ancient Church believed.

The historian Socrates (Church History VII.32) also reports that Origen discussed the title at length in the third century, in his Commentary on Romans. That part of the commentary does not survive, so the report cannot be checked.

That Mary conceived as a virgin is confessed by every historic Christian tradition, and most Protestants hold it firmly. The question here is what Mary's own words at the Annunciation reveal about her intention.

Matthew 1:18
"Now the birth of Jesus Christ was as follows: After His mother Mary was betrothed to Joseph, before they came together, she was found with child of the Holy Spirit."
🔵 Shared reading: The conception is by the Holy Spirit, before Joseph and Mary came together.
Luke 1:34
"Then Mary said to the angel, 'How can this be, since I do not know a man?'"
🔵 Orthodox reading: Mary is betrothed. If she meant to live with Joseph as his wife, a promise of a son would be no puzzle. Her question makes most sense if she had already given her virginity to God.
🟣 Protestant reading: Mary is simply asking how she can conceive now, before the marriage is completed.
St. Augustine of Hippo
Of Holy Virginity 4 (c. AD 401)
"How, says she, shall this be, seeing I know not a man? Which assuredly she would not say, unless she had before vowed herself unto God as a virgin."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Augustine reads Mary's question as the sign of a vow already made, and Gregory of Nyssa in the East reads it the same way. Not every Father reads the verse like this, but the reading goes back to the fourth century. If the vow is real, her virginity after the birth is not a later idea added to the story. It is the life she had already chosen before the angel came.

The ancient Church also confessed that Christ's birth left His mother's virginity unharmed. Christ, who later came to His disciples "when the doors were shut" (John 20:19), came forth from her without opening the gate.

Ezekiel 44:2
"And the Lord said to me, 'This gate shall be shut; it shall not be opened, and no man shall enter by it, because the Lord God of Israel has entered by it; therefore it shall be shut."
🔵 Orthodox reading: The Fathers and the Coptic Theotokia read the shut gate as the Virgin. Notice the reason the prophet gives: the gate stays shut "because the Lord God of Israel has entered by it." Section X returns to this.
🟣 Protestant reading: The verse describes the east gate of Ezekiel's visionary temple, shut in honour of the Lord's glory that entered by it (Ezekiel 43:4).

This is the heart of the question. The Fathers did not agree on every detail about the brothers of the Lord. Some held they were Joseph's sons by an earlier marriage; Jerome held they were cousins and rejected the earlier-marriage view. But on the point at issue they speak with one voice: Mary had no child but Jesus. What follows is in their own words.

Origen of Alexandria
Commentary on Matthew X.17 (c. AD 246)
"But some say, basing it on a tradition in the Gospel according to Peter, as it is entitled, or The Book of James, that the brethren of Jesus were sons of Joseph by a former wife, whom he married before Mary. Now those who say so wish to preserve the honour of Mary in virginity to the end, so that that body of hers which was appointed to minister to the Word which said, The Holy Ghost shall come upon you, and the power of the Most High shall overshadow you, might not know intercourse with a man after that the Holy Ghost came into her and the power from on high overshadowed her. And I think it in harmony with reason that Jesus was the first-fruit among men of the purity which consists in chastity, and Mary among women; for it were not pious to ascribe to any other than to her the first-fruit of virginity."

What the Witnesses Show

These writers lived in Alexandria, Cyprus, Cappadocia, Gaul, Antioch, Constantinople, Bethlehem, Pettau, North Africa and Palestine. They wrote in Greek and Latin, from the third century to the eighth. They disagree about who exactly the brothers were, which shows they were not copying one formula. Yet not one of them says that Mary had other children, and they say plainly and repeatedly that she did not. Chrysostom and Augustine treat the idea as unthinkable; Jerome refuses it because Scripture never says it; Origen calls the honour of her virginity the view of those "with sound mind." The only early writers who taught otherwise are named in Section XVII, and none of them is a Father of the Church.

Does the title Theotokos by itself require that Mary had no other children? Strictly, no. Ephesus defined the title to say who Christ is, and no council or Father defines it as including her lasting virginity. The homily attributed to St. Basil says openly that Mary's virginity was necessary up to the birth, and that what came afterwards would not have touched the teaching of the mystery (Section IX). Honesty requires saying so, and the case does not need more.

What the Fathers do is argue from her divine motherhood to her lasting virginity, as a matter of what is fitting and holy. The connection is not a syllogism but it is not an afterthought either. Here it is in their own words.

The Body That Served the Word

Origen, in the passage quoted in Section IX, gives the reason those who hold the Ever-Virgin do so: "that that body of hers which was appointed to minister to the Word... might not know intercourse with a man after that the Holy Ghost came into her." The body that bore the Word is not returned to common use.

Such a Mother

Chrysostom reasons the same way: "she having so become a mother, and having been counted worthy of a new sort of travail, and a child-bearing so strange," Joseph could never "have endured to know her." It is precisely because of whom she bore that he will not hear of it.

St. John of Damascus
Exposition of the Orthodox Faith IV.14 (c. AD 743)
"For could it be possible that she, who had borne God and from experience of the subsequent events had come to know the miracle, should receive the embrace of a man. God forbid! It is not the part of a chaste mind to think such thoughts, far less to commit such acts."
Byzantine WriterVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

This is the clearest statement of the link. The reason given is not a general rule about mothers. It is that she "had borne God."

The Gate the Lord Entered

Ezekiel 44:2
"And the Lord said to me, 'This gate shall be shut; it shall not be opened, and no man shall enter by it, because the Lord God of Israel has entered by it; therefore it shall be shut."
🔵 Orthodox reading: The gate is shut, and the prophet gives the reason: "because the Lord God of Israel has entered by it." The Fathers saw in this the Virgin through whom God came into the world. Her closing is the result of His entering.
🟣 Protestant reading: The verse is about the east gate of the temple in Ezekiel's vision, not about Mary. Its application to her is typology.

"Christ Himself is a virgin; and His mother is also a virgin; yea, though she is His mother, she is a virgin still. ... The Love of the Faithful

The Basil homily rests its case on the faithful: those who love Christ will not bear to hear that the Theotokos ever ceased to be a virgin. That is not a weak argument. It is the instinct of the whole Church, which saw in the God-bearer a vessel wholly given to God.

Where That Leaves the Question

Put plainly: a person can, in logic, call Mary the Mother of God and still say she had other children. But that is not what any Father of the Church said. The Church that fought for the title Theotokos confessed her the Ever-Virgin in the same breath, and its Fathers explained her lasting virginity by appeal to the God she bore. The Theotokos and the Ever-Virgin are not two separate doctrines that happen to sit side by side. In the mind of the Fathers, the second is the fitting fruit of the first.

The text most often cited against Mary's lasting virginity is Matthew 1:25. If Joseph did not know her "till" she gave birth, surely he did afterwards?

Matthew 1:25
"and did not know her till she had brought forth her firstborn Son. And he called His name Jesus."
🔵 Orthodox reading: Matthew is guarding the virgin conception: Joseph had no part in it. "Till" tells us what was true up to the birth and says nothing about afterwards.
🟣 Protestant reading: "Till" naturally implies that things changed after the birth.

How Scripture Uses "Till"

Matthew 28:20
"teaching them to observe all things that I have commanded you; and lo, I am with you always, even to the end of the age.' Amen."
🔵 Orthodox reading: The Greek is heos, the same word as in Matthew 1:25. Christ does not leave us when the age ends.
1 Corinthians 15:25
"For He must reign till He has put all enemies under His feet."
🔵 Orthodox reading: Paul uses achri, another Greek word for "until." Christ's reign does not end when His enemies are under His feet. "Till" marks a point without implying a change after it.
2 Samuel 6:23
"Therefore Michal the daughter of Saul had no children to the day of her death."
🔵 Orthodox reading: The Greek Old Testament reads heos, "until" the day of her death. No one supposes Michal had children afterwards.
Genesis 8:7
"Then he sent out a raven, which kept going to and fro until the waters had dried up from the earth."
🔵 Orthodox reading: Chrysostom points out that the Greek says the raven did not return "till" the waters were dried up; and it never returned at all.

Exodus 13:2
"'Consecrate to Me all the firstborn, whatever opens the womb among the children of Israel, both of man and beast; it is Mine.'"
🔵 Orthodox reading: The firstborn had to be consecrated as soon as he opened the womb. Parents could not wait to see whether other children followed. An only child was a firstborn child.
St. Jerome
Against Helvidius 12 (c. AD 383)
"Our position is this: Every only begotten son is a first-born son, but not every first-born is an only begotten. By first-born we understand not only one who is succeeded by others, but one who has had no predecessor."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

An ancient Jewish grave inscription makes the same point. Found at Tell el-Yehudiyeh in Egypt and dated to about 5 BC, it remembers a woman named Arsinoe who died giving birth to her "firstborn" child. She had no second child. "Firstborn" was simply the name for the first child to open the womb.

Mark 6:3
"Is this not the carpenter, the Son of Mary, and brother of James, Joses, Judas, and Simon? And are not His sisters here with us?' So they were offended at Him."
🔵 Orthodox reading: Jesus alone is called "the Son of Mary." The four men are called His brothers, but never her sons.
🟣 Protestant reading: The plain reading is that these are Mary's other children.

Three explanations have been given in the history of the Church.

Joseph's Sons by an Earlier Marriage

Joseph was a widower with children when he was entrusted with Mary. The brothers are Jesus' brothers through Joseph, not Mary's sons. Found in the Protoevangelium of James and held by Origen, Eusebius, Hilary and Epiphanius. The view of the Oriental Orthodox Churches.

Cousins

The brothers were sons of Mary's kinswoman, "Mary the wife of Clopas," and so cousins of Jesus. Argued by Jerome, who held that Joseph too remained a virgin. The usual view in the West.

Mary's Later Sons

The brothers were children of Mary and Joseph born after Jesus. Argued by Helvidius around 383 and refuted by Jerome. Rejected by the ancient Church; common among Protestants today.

The Protoevangelium of James
Chapter 9 (c. AD 150)
"And the priest said to Joseph, You have been chosen by lot to take into your keeping the virgin of the Lord. But Joseph refused, saying: I have children, and I am an old man, and she is a young girl."
Early Christian WritingVERBATIM · ANF TRANSLATION

The Protoevangelium is not Scripture, and the Church does not read it as Scripture. But it shows that within about a century of the apostles, Christians already explained the brothers of the Lord as Joseph's children and already believed Mary remained a virgin. Origen names it as a source for this view (Section IX).

Both ancient views agree on the essential point: the brothers were not Mary's sons. Jerome sharply rejected the earlier-marriage view, and still he and the Fathers who held it stood together against Helvidius.

Hebrew and Aramaic have no separate word for cousin. The Hebrew ach, brother, covers half-brothers, nephews and other kinsmen, and the Greek Old Testament translates it with adelphos. The Gospel writers, steeped in that Greek Bible, use the word the same way.

Genesis 14:14
"Now when Abram heard that his brother was taken captive, he armed his three hundred and eighteen trained servants who were born in his own house, and went in pursuit as far as Dan."
🔵 Orthodox reading: Lot was Abram's nephew, the son of his brother Haran (Genesis 11:27), yet Scripture calls him Abram's brother.
Genesis 13:8
"So Abram said to Lot, 'Please let there be no strife between you and me, and between my herdsmen and your herdsmen; for we are brethren."
🔵 Orthodox reading: Uncle and nephew call each other brethren.
1 Chronicles 23:22
"And Eleazar died, and had no sons, but only daughters; and their brethren, the sons of Kish, took them as wives."
🔵 Orthodox reading: The sons of Kish were cousins of Eleazar's daughters (verse 21), yet they are called "their brethren."
Colossians 4:10
"Aristarchus my fellow prisoner greets you, with Mark the cousin of Barnabas (about whom you received instructions: if he comes to you, welcome him),"
🔵 Orthodox reading: Greek does have a word for cousin, anepsios, and Paul uses it here.
🟣 Protestant reading: If the evangelists meant cousins, why not use anepsios?

The Protestant question is fair, and the answer is that the evangelists are following the Greek Bible's way of naming kin, and the early Church's way of naming the Lord's family. The word "brother" alone cannot settle the question. What settles it is who the brothers' mother was, which the cross scene answers (Section XVI), and the unbroken witness of the Fathers (Section IX).

Galatians 1:19
"But I saw none of the other apostles except James, the Lord's brother."
🔵 Orthodox reading: "The Lord's brother" was James' title in the early Church, to set him apart from James the son of Zebedee. It does not say whose son he was.
🟣 Protestant reading: Paul means a real brother, a son of Mary.
Hegesippus, in Eusebius
Church History II.23 (c. AD 170, quoted c. AD 325)
"James, the brother of the Lord, succeeded to the government of the Church in conjunction with the apostles. He has been called the Just by all from the time of our Saviour to the present day; for there were many that bore the name of James. He was holy from his mother's womb; and he drank no wine nor strong drink, nor did he eat flesh."
Church HistorianVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION
Eusebius of Caesarea
Church History II.1 (c. AD 325)
"This James was called the brother of the Lord because he was known as a son of Joseph, and Joseph was supposed to be the father of Christ, because the Virgin, being betrothed to him, was found with child by the Holy Ghost before they came together"
Church HistorianVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Eusebius, the first great historian of the Church, gives the reason for the title outright: James was known as Joseph's son. That is the view the Coptic Church holds. Jerome, by contrast, identified this James with "James the son of Alphaeus" among the Twelve (Matthew 10:3) and took him to be a cousin. On either view he was not Mary's son.

To be fair to the other side, Hegesippus also speaks of Jude as "the Lord's brother according to the flesh" (Eusebius, Church History III.20). Protestants cite this phrase. But "according to the flesh" distinguishes earthly kinship from spiritual brotherhood; it does not say whose son Jude was. The same Hegesippus says that Symeon, the next bishop of Jerusalem, was the son of Clopas, the Lord's uncle (below), and Eusebius speaks of Jude as one of "the so-called brothers of the Saviour" (III.32).

Eusebius of Caesarea, citing Hegesippus
Church History III.11 (c. AD 325)
"They all with one consent pronounced Symeon, the son of Clopas, of whom the Gospel also makes mention; to be worthy of the episcopal throne of that parish. He was a cousin, as they say, of the Saviour. For Hegesippus records that Clopas was a brother of Joseph."
Church HistorianVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION
John 7:5
"For even His brothers did not believe in Him."
🔵 Orthodox reading: During the ministry the brothers did not believe. After the resurrection the Lord appeared to James (1 Corinthians 15:7), and the brothers are found praying with the apostles (Acts 1:14).

The scene at the cross is the strongest single piece of evidence in the Gospels that Mary had no other sons. It works in two steps.

Step One: Christ Gives His Mother to John

John 19:25-27
"Now there stood by the cross of Jesus His mother, and His mother's sister, Mary the wife of Clopas, and Mary Magdalene. When Jesus therefore saw His mother, and the disciple whom He loved standing by, He said to His mother, 'Woman, behold your son!' Then He said to the disciple, 'Behold your mother!' And from that hour that disciple took her to his own home."
🔵 Orthodox reading: The care of a widowed mother fell to her sons. If James, Joses, Judas and Simon were her own sons, passing them over for a disciple from another family would make little sense and would shame them in public. Christ gives her to John because she had no other son to receive her.
🟣 Protestant reading: The brothers did not yet believe (John 7:5), so Christ entrusted her to a believer.

The Protestant answer does not hold. Within weeks the brothers are with the apostles in the upper room, praying "with Mary the mother of Jesus" (Acts 1:14), and James soon leads the Church in Jerusalem. If they were her sons, the Lord who knew all things would hardly have passed over them for a few weeks of unbelief. The Fathers saw the point at once.

Origen of Alexandria
Commentary on John I.6 (c. AD 230)
"For if Mary, as those declare who with sound mind extol her, had no other son but Jesus, and yet Jesus says to His mother, Woman, behold your son, and not Behold you have this son also..."

St. John Chrysostom

"For if he had known her, and had kept her in the place of a wife, how is it that our Lord commits her, as unprotected, and having no one, to His disciple, and commands him to take her to his own home?"
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Step Two: The Other Mary at the Cross

Matthew 27:55-56
"And many women who followed Jesus from Galilee, ministering to Him, were there looking on from afar, among whom were Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James and Joses, and the mother of Zebedee's sons."
🔵 Orthodox reading: Two of the Lord's brothers are named James and Joses (Mark 6:3). Here a woman at the cross is "the mother of James and Joses," and she is plainly not the Virgin, whom the evangelists would call "His mother."
Mark 15:40
"There were also women looking on from afar, among whom were Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James the Less and of Joses, and Salome,"
🔵 Orthodox reading: Mark calls her the mother of "James the Less and of Joses." The brothers named in Mark 6:3 have a mother at the cross who is not the Virgin Mary.

John's list at the cross includes "Mary the wife of Clopas" (John 19:25), and Hegesippus says Clopas was Joseph's brother (Section XV). Many, from Jerome onward, identify her with "the mother of James and Joses." Others count her separately. Either way, the James and Joses who are called the Lord's brothers had a mother who was not the Virgin Mary. The Gospels themselves, read side by side, rule out the claim that they were her sons.

It is often said that Mary's perpetual virginity is a medieval invention. The record shows the opposite: the belief is attested by the middle of the second century, it was held by all the Fathers, the few early writers who denied it were answered and censured, and even the Reformers kept it. Its abandonment is the late development.

c. AD 150
The Protoevangelium of James presents Joseph as an elderly widower with children, and Mary as a consecrated virgin.
c. AD 200
Tertullian in Carthage argues that Mary bore other children after Jesus. He later joins the Montanist sect, and the Church never receives him as a Father.
c. AD 230–246
Origen in Alexandria and Caesarea teaches that Mary "had no other son but Jesus," and calls this the view of those who honour her "with sound mind."
c. AD 300
Victorinus of Pettau, bishop and martyr, speaks of the Lord's brothers as kinsmen, not sons of Mary (as reported by Jerome).
c. AD 340
St. Athanasius calls Mary "Ever-Virgin" and "Bearer of God" in his Discourses Against the Arians.
c. AD 377
St. Epiphanius writes against the "Antidicomarianites" in Arabia, who said Mary lived with Joseph as his wife after the birth of Christ.
c. AD 383
Helvidius in Rome argues that Mary had other children. St. Jerome answers him in Against Helvidius.
c. AD 390–393
Jovinian, who denied that Mary remained a virgin in giving birth, is condemned by synods at Rome under Pope Siricius and at Milan under St. Ambrose.
c. AD 391–392
Bonosus, a bishop in Illyricum, teaches that Mary had other children. His case is referred by the Council of Capua to the bishops of Macedonia, and he is condemned.
AD 431
The Council of Ephesus upholds the title Theotokos against Nestorius.
AD 553
The Second Council of Constantinople, received by the Chalcedonian churches, names Mary "ever-virgin" in its anathemas.
16th century
Luther, Zwingli and Calvin all reject the Helvidian view, and the Reformed Second Helvetic Confession (1566) confesses Christ "born of Mary ever virgin" (see below).
18th–19th centuries
Belief in Mary's perpetual virginity fades among Protestants, and by the nineteenth century the Helvidian view is common.

Those Who Denied It

The early writers known to have taught that Mary had other children are Tertullian, the Antidicomarianites, Helvidius and Bonosus. Each was opposed by the Church of his own day.

St. Jerome
Against Helvidius 19 (c. AD 383)
"Of Tertullian I say no more than that he did not belong to the Church."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Jerome's verdict on Tertullian is blunt. Tertullian was a brilliant early writer, but he left the communion of the Church for the Montanists, and the Church never counted him among her Fathers. His opinion on this question is the opinion of one man, and the Church rejected it.

Nestorius, sometimes named in these debates, is not known to have denied Mary's lasting virginity. His quarrel was over the title Theotokos, not her virginity.

The Second Helvetic Confession, written by Heinrich Bullinger, Zwingli's successor at Zurich, became one of the most widely received Reformed confessions. In its chapter on Christ it confesses Him "born of Mary ever virgin."

Calvin is more cautious than Luther and Zwingli. He calls the question one that should not be pressed out of curiosity, and he does not argue for the doctrine at length. But he rejects Helvidius' argument outright. None of the three Reformers taught that Mary had other children.

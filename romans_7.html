Romans 7: The Law & the Divided Self

"But Christ saved even one that fled from Him. See what a vast advantage grace has!" St John Chrysostom, Homily 13 on Romans

Romans 7 is the most personal chapter Paul ever wrote, and perhaps the most argued over. "For what I will to do, that I do not practice; but what I hate, that I do... O wretched man that I am! Who will deliver me from this body of death?" Almost every Christian recognises himself in these words. The question is whether Paul meant them to describe the Christian.

St John Chrysostom, and most of the Greek Fathers, read the chapter as a portrait of humanity under the law and before grace: a person who knows the good and delights in it, but has no power to do it until Christ comes. St Augustine read it that way too, at first, and then changed his mind and took it as the struggle of a mature Christian. Luther and Calvin followed the later Augustine, and the idea that the believer is "at once righteous and a sinner" grew partly from this chapter.

The question is not academic. It shapes how a Christian understands his own struggle with sin: as the normal and permanent state of the believer in this life, or as something that grace in Christ, given in Romans 8, really overcomes. This page lays out the readings fairly, including an Egyptian desert father who offers a third view, and shows why the Orthodox answer holds both the honesty of the struggle and the reality of the victory.

"For I delight in it, and consent to it, and flee for refuge to it, yet still it had not the power of saving one who had fled to it. But Christ saved even one that fled from Him." St John Chrysostom, Homily 13 on Romans

Law
νόμος · nomos
Usually the law of Moses, but in 7:21–25 Paul also speaks of a "law" in the sense of a ruling principle: "the law of sin" and "the law of my mind."
Commandment
ἐντολή · entolē
A single command, here "You shall not covet" (7:7).
Flesh
σάρξ · sarx
Not the body as such, but human nature as weak, mortal and open to sin. "In the flesh" means living apart from the Spirit (7:5).
Carnal, fleshly
σάρκινος · sarkinos
Made of flesh, weak. "I am carnal, sold under sin" (7:14).
Spiritual
πνευματικός · pneumatikos
Of the Spirit, from God. "The law is spiritual" (7:14).
Desire, lust
ἐπιθυμία · epithymia
Strong desire, here wrong desire, coveting (7:7–8).
Passions
παθήματα · pathēmata
"The sinful passions" (7:5). In the Orthodox ascetic tradition, the passions are disordered desires that the Christian struggles to heal.
Inward man
ὁ ἔσω ἄνθρωπος · ho esō anthrōpos
The inner self, mind and heart, which delights in God's law (7:22).
Mind
νοῦς · nous
The mind or understanding. In Orthodox spirituality, the nous is the eye of the soul, the place of prayer and the knowledge of God.
Wretched
ταλαίπωρος · talaipōros
Miserable, distressed. "O wretched man that I am!" (7:24).
Romans 7:1-3
"Or do you not know, brethren (for I speak to those who know the law), that the law has dominion over a man as long as he lives? For the woman who has a husband is bound by the law to her husband as long as he lives. But if the husband dies, she is released from the law of her husband. So then if, while her husband lives, she marries another man, she will be called an adulteress; but if her husband dies, she is free from that law, so that she is no adulteress, though she has married another man."
🔵 Orthodox reading: A law binds only while a person lives. When a husband dies, his wife is free to marry another.
Romans 7:4
"Therefore, my brethren, you also have become dead to the law through the body of Christ, that you may be married to another—to Him who was raised from the dead, that we should bear fruit to God."
🔵 Orthodox reading: Through Christ's body we died to the law, so that we might belong to another, the risen Christ, and bear fruit for God.
Romans 7:5-6
"For when we were in the flesh, the sinful passions which were aroused by the law were at work in our members to bear fruit to death. But now we have been delivered from the law, having died to what we were held by, so that we should serve in the newness of the Spirit and not in the oldness of the letter."
🔵 Orthodox reading: The law could stir up sinful passions but not heal them. Now we serve in the newness of the Spirit, not the oldness of the letter.

Paul's image is a marriage. We were once bound to the law; through baptism into Christ's death (6:3–4), we died to it, and now we belong to Christ. The point is not that God's commandments no longer matter. The point is that we are no longer trying to be righteous by the law's power, which was never able to give life, but are joined to the living Christ, whose Spirit bears fruit in us.

Chrysostom notices how careful Paul is not to insult the law even here. In the illustration it is the husband who dies, but in the application Paul says it is we who died to the law. He spares the law the indignity of being called dead.

Romans 7:7
"What shall we say then? Is the law sin? Certainly not! On the contrary, I would not have known sin except through the law. For I would not have known covetousness unless the law had said, 'You shall not covet.'"
🔵 Orthodox reading: The law is not sin. It shows sin up for what it is. "You shall not covet" revealed the coveting already in the heart.
Romans 7:9-11
"I was alive once without the law, but when the commandment came, sin revived and I died. And the commandment, which was to bring life, I found to bring death. For sin, taking occasion by the commandment, deceived me, and by it killed me."
🔵 Orthodox reading: Sin used the good commandment as a beachhead, and deceived and killed.
Romans 7:12
"Therefore the law is holy, and the commandment holy and just and good."
🔵 Orthodox reading: The law is holy, and the commandment holy, just and good.
St John Chrysostom
Homily 13 on Romans (on 7:14)
"For by calling it spiritual, he shows it to be a teacher of virtue and hostile to vice; for this is what being spiritual means, leading off from sin of every kind. And this the Law did do, by frightening, admonishing, chastening, correcting, recommending every kind of virtue. Whence then, was sin produced, if the teacher was so admirable? It was from the listlessness of its disciples."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Here Orthodox and Protestants stand together. The law of God is good. The fault lies in us, not in the commandment. Both traditions have had to resist people who despised the Old Testament or set Christ against the law, from Marcion in the second century onward. Jesus came not to destroy the law but to fulfil it (Matthew 5:17).

Where they differ in emphasis is the law's role after conversion. Lutheran theology especially stresses the law's work of showing sin and driving us to Christ. Reformed theology gives special weight to a "third use" of the law as a guide for the believer's life. The Orthodox would say that the commandments, written now on the heart by the Spirit (Jeremiah 31:33), are the shape of the life of love, and are kept not by our own strength but by grace.

Romans 7:18-20
"For I know that in me (that is, in my flesh) nothing good dwells; for to will is present with me, but how to perform what is good I do not find. For the good that I will to do, I do not do; but the evil I will not to do, that I practice. Now if I do what I will not to do, it is no longer I who do it, but sin that dwells in me."
🔵 Orthodox reading: "In my flesh nothing good dwells." Paul is describing what human nature can do left to itself, not condemning the body God made.

In Chrysostom's day the Manichees taught that matter and the body were evil, the creation of an evil god. They loved Romans 7. Chrysostom answered them:

St John Chrysostom
Homily 13 on Romans (on 7:18)
"For the fact that no good thing dwells in it, does not show that it is evil itself. Now we admit, that the flesh is not so great as the soul, and is inferior to it, yet not contrary, or opposed to it, or evil; but that it is beneath the soul, as a harp beneath a harper, and as a ship under the pilot."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION
St John Chrysostom
Homily 13 on Romans (on 7:23)
"But such doctrines as these are not the Church's, for it is the sin only that she condemns; and both the Laws which God has given, both that of nature and that of Moses, she says are hostile to this, and not to the flesh; for the flesh she denies to be sin, for it is a work of God's, and one very useful too in order to virtue, if we live soberly."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION
St John Chrysostom
Homily 13 on Romans (on 7:14)
"For in themselves they were not sin, but, when their extravagancy was unbridled, it wrought this effect. Thus (that I may take one of them and examine it as a specimen) desire is not sin: but when it has run into extravagance, being not minded to keep within the laws of marriage, but springing even upon other men's wives; then the thing henceforward becomes adultery, yet not by reason of the desire, but by reason of its exorbitancy."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

This is an important point of difference with the Augustinian and Reformation tradition. Augustine taught that concupiscence, disordered desire, remains in the baptised and can be called sin, because it comes from sin and inclines to it, though its guilt is taken away. The Lutheran and Reformed confessions go further: the Westminster Confession calls the remaining corruption and "all the motions thereof... truly and properly sin." Chrysostom says that the natural desires God gave are not sin; sin is their disorder and excess, when the will consents. The Orthodox ascetic tradition, which distinguishes carefully between a passing thought, consent and action, follows Chrysostom.

1. Humanity under the law: St John Chrysostom

Chrysostom says that with the words "but I am carnal" Paul begins "giving us a sketch now of man, as comporting himself in the Law, and before the Law." The "I" is Paul speaking for all who lived without the grace of Christ:

St John Chrysostom
Homily 13 on Romans (on 7:14)
"For when he says, sold under sin, he means it not of those who were under the Law only, but of those who had lived before the Law also, and of men from the very first."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION
St John Chrysostom
Homily 13 on Romans (on 7:24)
"Do you notice what a great thraldom that of vice is, in that it overcomes even a mind that delighted in the Law? For no one can rejoin, he means, that I hate the Law and abhor it, and so sin overcomes me. For I delight in it, and consent to it, and flee for refuge to it, yet still it had not the power of saving one who had fled to it. But Christ saved even one that fled from Him. 3. The saints and their thoughts: Abba Theonas in Egypt

St John Cassian, who learned the monastic life in the Egyptian desert, records a conference with Abba Theonas that takes a third path. Theonas agrees with Augustine that the passage describes holy people, not gross sinners, but he says the "evil" the saints cannot escape is not adultery or theft but the wandering of the mind away from God in prayer:

St John Cassian (Abba Theonas)
Conferences XXIII.1
"...these passages on the contrary plainly show that they cannot possibly fit the person of sinners, but that what is said can only apply to those that are perfect, and that it only suits the chastity of those who follow the good example of the Apostles."
Desert FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION
St John Cassian (Abba Theonas)
Conferences XXIII.10
"For they feel that they cannot perform the good that they would, but are ever falling into the evil which they would not, and which they hate, i.e., wandering thoughts and care for carnal things."

Chrysostom and the Greek Fathers

The "I" is humanity under the law and before grace, knowing the good but powerless to do it. Romans 8 describes the Christian, set free by the Spirit.

Later Augustine, Luther, Calvin

The "I" is the mature Christian, still fighting the remnants of sin. The believer is righteous in Christ and yet a sinner in himself until death.

Abba Theonas (Cassian)

The "I" is the saint, who no longer falls into grave sin but cannot yet hold his mind on God without distraction, and longs for perfect union with Him.

Modern scholars are still divided. Since W. G. Kümmel's study of 1929, many have read the "I" as a rhetorical figure for humanity or Israel under the law, close to Chrysostom. Others, such as C. E. B. Cranfield and James Dunn, defend the Christian reading. The strongest argument for the Christian view is the present tense and the delight in God's law. The strongest argument against it is that the man of 7:14 is "sold under sin," while the Christian of 6:18 has been "set free from sin," and that 8:2 says the Spirit "has made me free from the law of sin and death," using the very phrase of 7:23.

Does "what I will to do, that I do not practice" mean the will is enslaved and cannot choose? Chrysostom is careful to say no:

St John Chrysostom
Homily 13 on Romans (on 7:15)
"Whence it appears that he says, that I would not, not as denying free will, or as adducing any constrained necessity. For if it was not willingly, but by compulsion, that we sinned, then the punishments that took place before would not be justifiable."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION
St John Chrysostom
Homily 13 on Romans (on 7:19–20)
"Now the essence of the soul and body and of that choice are not the same, for the two first are God's works, and the other is a motion from ourselves towards whatever we please to direct it. For willing is indeed natural, and is from God: but willing on this wise is our own, and from our own mind."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Calvin agrees that sin is voluntary and not compelled, but draws a stronger conclusion about the natural man:

John Calvin
Commentary on Romans (on 7:14)
"Compulsion I always except, for we sin spontaneously, as it would be no sin, were it not voluntary. But we are so given up to sin, that we can do willingly nothing but sin; for the corruption which bears rule within us thus drives us onward."
ReformerVERBATIM · OWEN TRANSLATION (1849)

Both reject the idea that we sin under compulsion. The difference is whether fallen man can do anything but sin. Chrysostom's man in Romans 7 still "delights" in the good and hates the evil; his will is weak and overpowered, but not dead. Calvin's natural man "can do willingly nothing but sin." The Orthodox stand with Chrysostom: the will is wounded and cannot save itself, but it can still turn toward God when grace calls, and grace does not override it but heals it. This is the ground of the discussion of Romans 9.

Galatians 5:16-17
"I say then: Walk in the Spirit, and you shall not fulfill the lust of the flesh. For the flesh lusts against the Spirit, and the Spirit against the flesh; and these are contrary to one another, so that you do not do the things that you wish."
🔵 Orthodox reading: The flesh and the Spirit are at war, in the Christian too. But the command is to walk in the Spirit, and the promise is that we will not fulfil the desires of the flesh.
1 John 1:8-9
"If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us. If we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness."
🔵 Orthodox reading: No Christian is sinless. But confession brings real forgiveness and real cleansing.
Romans 8:1-2
"There is therefore now no condemnation to those who are in Christ Jesus, who do not walk according to the flesh, but according to the Spirit. For the law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus has made me free from the law of sin and death."
🔵 Orthodox reading: The answer to Romans 7 comes at once: no condemnation in Christ, and freedom from the law of sin and death through the Spirit.

Whatever one decides about the "I" of Romans 7, every tradition agrees on two things. Christians still struggle with sin, and the answer is Christ. The difference is in what one expects of that struggle.

The Orthodox tradition does not pretend the struggle ends at baptism. The Philokalia, the great collection of Orthodox writings on prayer, is largely about the war with thoughts and passions that Abba Theonas described. But it never treats defeat as normal. Its whole aim is healing: the passions can be quieted, the mind can be gathered in prayer, and the saints are living proof that grace really transforms. That is why Chrysostom insists that Romans 8, not Romans 7, is the picture of the Christian life: "the law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus has made me free."

The concern Orthodox readers have about "at once righteous and a sinner" is not that it is too honest about sin, but that, taken as a permanent description, it can make the victory of Romans 8 seem out of reach. The concern Protestants have about the Orthodox view is that it can make Christians anxious about their progress. Both concerns are worth hearing. The Orthodox answer is the Jesus Prayer: "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner," prayed by people who are being made saints.

OO Response: Paul also uses the past tense in 7:7–13 ("I was alive once without the law") of a time he cannot literally remember, and Augustine himself first read the passage as describing humanity under the law. The present tense makes the picture vivid; it does not settle who is speaking.

And the description does not fit what Paul says of Christians elsewhere. The Christian is "set free from sin" (6:18); the man of Romans 7 is "sold under sin" (7:14). The Holy Spirit, named some twenty times in Romans 8, is not named once in 7:14–25.

OO Response: The Orthodox pray "have mercy on me, a sinner" every day and go to confession for life. Abba Theonas, a saint of the Egyptian desert, insisted that even the holiest are not without sin. The Church does not teach sinless perfection in this life.

What she teaches is that grace really changes us, so that sin no longer rules (6:14) and the saints grow in holiness. That is not perfectionism; it is Romans 6 and 8.

OO Response: Chrysostom says desire becomes sin "by reason of its exorbitancy," when it breaks out of God's order. The Orthodox ascetic writers are severe about thoughts and desires, teaching that a thought welcomed and entertained already defiles the heart (Matthew 5:28).

The difference is that they do not call the bare natural desire, which God created, sin in itself. That protects the goodness of creation and the body, which Romans 7 is often misused to attack.

OO Response: Augustine changed his mind during the Pelagian controversy, when he needed to show that even the holiest Christians depend wholly on grace. That was a real truth, and the Orthodox affirm it.

But Chrysostom and the Greek tradition read Paul in his own language and never adopted the later Augustinian reading. A change of mind in the heat of controversy is not automatically the better reading, and Augustine's own first view remains a witness on the other side.

Paul calls himself the chief of sinners (1 Timothy 1:15) and says he disciplines his body lest he be disqualified (1 Corinthians 9:27). He knew struggle. But he also says, "I have fought the good fight" (2 Timothy 4:7) and "imitate me, just as I also imitate Christ" (1 Corinthians 11:1). The Fathers saw in him both humility and real holiness.

As the cry of every person who has tried to be good by his own strength and failed, and as the doorway to Romans 8. The right response to "O wretched man that I am!" is not despair but "I thank God, through Jesus Christ our Lord," followed by prayer, repentance, confession and the Eucharist, through which the Spirit gives life.

Christians are not bound by the ceremonial law of Moses, such as circumcision and the food laws (Acts 15). The moral law, summed up in love of God and neighbour, is fulfilled in us by the Spirit (Romans 8:4; 13:8–10). See Sabbath & the Lord's Day.

"There is therefore now no condemnation to those who are in Christ Jesus." Romans 8 is the answer to Romans 7: life in the Spirit, adoption, the groaning of creation and the love from which nothing can separate us. That is Romans 8.

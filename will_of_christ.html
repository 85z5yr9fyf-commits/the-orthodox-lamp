THE ORTHODOX LAMP

One Will or Two? The Will of the Incarnate Word

"Not as I will, but as You will." Matthew 26:39

✦ ✦ ✦
Home›Oriental Orthodoxy›The Will of Christ
Deyrul ZafaranSyriac Orthodox · Mardin

Ask an Eastern Orthodox, Roman Catholic or Reformed Christian what the Oriental Orthodox believe about the will of Christ and the answer is usually the same: they are monothelites, they deny that Christ had a human will, and the Sixth Council condemned them for it. The first two claims are simply mistaken. The third is half true and badly misread: the Sixth Council did name Severus, but every man it condemned for monothelitism was a Chalcedonian.

The Oriental Orthodox Church does not deny that the incarnate Word has a true and complete human will. What she refuses to do is to count His willing as two, because the one who wills is one, God made man. She confesses one will of the incarnate Word, composite and theandric, in which the natural divine will and the natural human will are united without mixture, confusion, separation or division. That is the same grammar by which she confesses one incarnate nature of the Word, and it stands or falls with St. Cyril.

This page sets out what is actually confessed, examines the central texts (Gethsemane above all), tells the seventh century story accurately, and answers the standard objections. It draws on the case made by Oriental Orthodox apologists, including Agen of Apostolic Orthodoxy and Fr. Peter Farrington, and on the joint statements in which both Orthodox families have already said much of what follows together.

"O My Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from Me; nevertheless, not as I will, but as You will." Matthew 26:39

A Note on Quotations

Patristic and conciliar texts are given from public domain translations where possible and marked VERBATIM. Where a Father's documented teaching is summarised rather than reproduced, the card is marked PARAPHRASE. Where the only available translation is modern and copyrighted, as for much of Severus and all of Maximus, the text is paraphrased with the source named, so that the reader can go to it. Severus' Letter I and Dionysius' Letter IV are quoted from public domain translations of 1915 and 1897.

Thelema / Thelesis
θέλημα, θέλησις: "will, willing"
Both the faculty of willing and the act of willing. Much of the dispute dissolves once it is asked which of the two is being counted.
Energeia
ἐνέργεια: "activity, operation"
The activity proper to a nature. The seventh century controversy began over energy (monoenergism) before it moved to will (monothelitism).
Natural Will
θέλημα φυσικόν
The faculty of willing that belongs to a nature as such. Every rational nature has one. The Oriental Orthodox affirm that Christ's humanity possesses it fully.
Gnomic Will
γνώμη: "opinion, deliberation"
In St. Maximus, the deliberating mode of willing in which a human person weighs alternatives without certain knowledge of the good. Both families deny it of Christ, who never wavers.
Theandric
θεανδρικός: "divine-human"
From St. Dionysius: the new divine-human activity of God made man. The Oriental Orthodox apply it to the one will and energy of the incarnate Word.
Composite
σύνθετος: "put together"
Describes a union in which the united elements remain what they are. The Fifth Council calls the union synthetic; both families in 1990 called the hypostasis of the Logos composite.
Monothelitism
μόνος + θέλημα: "one will only"
The seventh century Chalcedonian imperial doctrine of one will in Christ, which in its opponents' judgment left His human nature without its own natural motion. Condemned in 681, and rejected by the Oriental Orthodox also.
Dyothelitism
δύο θελήματα: "two wills"
The teaching of the Sixth Council that there are two natural wills in Christ, the human following and subject to the divine.
Hypostasis
ὑπόστασις: "subsistence, person"
The concrete one who exists and acts. Both families agree that in Christ the one who wills is always the one hypostasis of the Logos incarnate.
Blameless Passion
ἀδιάβλητα πάθη
Natural human affections that are not sinful: hunger, thirst, weariness, grief, the shrinking from death. Christ took all of them as His own.

Three errors travel together and need separating, because each of them is believed by people who have never read a line of Severus.

1. "The Oriental Orthodox deny that Christ has a human will"

They do not, and never have. A humanity without a will would be an animated body moved from outside, which is Apollinarianism, and the Oriental Orthodox Fathers condemned Apollinarius by name. Severus insists repeatedly that the Word united to Himself flesh endowed with a rational and intelligent soul, and the rational soul is precisely the seat of willing. What is not assumed is not healed; the Oriental Orthodox have always said so.

2. "Monothelitism was the Oriental Orthodox position"

Monothelitism was a Chalcedonian invention. It was drafted in Constantinople, promoted by a Byzantine emperor, and condemned at the Sixth Council in the persons of Chalcedonian patriarchs and a pope of Rome. Its purpose was to win back the non-Chalcedonians by offering one will on top of two natures. The Oriental Orthodox Churches were never party to it, sent no one to the council that condemned it, and do not recognise its formula as their faith. The council did liken the heresy to the teaching of Severus; Section XII tells the story and shows why the comparison does not hold.

3. "Gethsemane proves two wills struggling inside Christ"

No Orthodox theologian on either side teaches that. The Sixth Council itself says the two wills are not contrary to one another and that the human will follows without resisting or reluctance. If anyone imagines two parties in Christ negotiating, he has left both Orthodox families and arrived at Nestorius.

So Where Is the Real Disagreement?

Once the misconceptions are cleared away, the real question is narrow: whether the will of the incarnate Word should be counted. The Chalcedonian tradition counts according to the natures and says two. The Oriental Orthodox tradition speaks according to the union and says one, because the one Son wills, and to count His willing is to risk dividing the one who wills. Both affirm the full divine and human faculty. The difference is about where the number belongs.

Oriental Orthodox

One composite, theandric will of the incarnate Word. The natural human will is fully present and active, united to the divine without confusion or division. The one who wills is always the one Son.

Chalcedonian (Dyothelite)

Two natural wills, divine and human, in the one hypostasis. Not contrary; the human follows and is subject to the divine. Defined at Constantinople III (681).

Monothelitism (Condemned)

Two natures but one will, a formula its opponents judged to leave the humanity without its own natural motion. A Byzantine compromise, condemned in 681 and rejected by both Orthodox families.

Nestorianism (Condemned)

Two willing subjects, the Word and the man Jesus, united by agreement of purpose. The error that both families are trying to avoid when they speak of the will of Christ.

The Oriental Orthodox confession about the will of Christ can be stated in five propositions. Each one is drawn from Severus, from St. Cyril whom he follows, or from the agreed statements to which the Oriental Orthodox Churches have put their names.

The Five Propositions

First, the divine nature of the Word has its natural will, which He shares with the Father and the Holy Spirit. There is one will in the Holy Trinity because there is one divine nature.

Second, the humanity the Word took from the Theotokos is complete, with a rational soul, and therefore with a natural human will. That will is not vestigial and not idle. It is the faculty in which the Word, as man, chooses the good and obeys the Father.

Third, the human will never acts independently, as if it marked the presence of another subject. It is the Word's own human will, exactly as the flesh is the Word's own flesh.

Fourth, the union is natural and hypostatic, so the divine and human wills are united without mixture, confusion, separation or division. They are distinguished in contemplation, and they remain what they are by nature.

Fifth, therefore the incarnate Word wills as one, in a composite and theandric way, and the Church speaks of one will of the incarnate Word in the same sense in which she speaks, with St. Cyril, of one incarnate nature of God the Word.

Why the Human Will Matters to Us

Severus does not merely concede a human will. He makes it the battlefield of salvation. Adam was defeated in his will; the second Adam must win in His. Commenting on the prophecy that Emmanuel would refuse the evil and choose the good, Severus argues that God as God has no need to choose the good, and that the Word took a soul endowed with will precisely so that our wills, bent toward evil, might be turned back toward choosing what is right. A Christ without a working human will could not undo Adam's fall, and Severus knew it.

St. Severus of Antioch
On the prophecy of Emmanuel (Isaiah 7:15), as cited by Fr. Peter Farrington
From the time He lay in swaddling clothes, before any age of discernment, the Emmanuel spurned the evil and chose the good. These words show that the Word united to Himself not flesh only but a soul endowed with will and understanding, so that our souls might lean toward choosing good. God as God does not need to choose the good; it was for our sake that He assumed this.
Oriental Orthodox SaintPARAPHRASE

This is the decisive point against the charge of monothelitism. The monothelite formula of the seventh century was condemned for leaving the humanity of Christ without its own motion. Severus, a century earlier, made the human will's own motion toward the good the very mechanism of our redemption.

The strongest argument for two wills runs like this. In the Holy Trinity there are three persons and one will, so will cannot belong to the person; it must belong to the nature. Christ has two natures, so Christ has two wills. The argument is old, it is serious, and it deserves a careful answer rather than a dismissal.

What the Argument Gets Right

The faculty of willing is natural. Every rational nature has one, and the Father, Son and Holy Spirit have one will because they have one nature. The Oriental Orthodox do not dispute this. It is exactly why they insist that Christ's humanity has its own natural will: being human, it must.

What the Argument Skips

There is a difference between the faculty by which someone wills and the one who wills. Natures do not will. Persons will, by means of the faculties their nature gives them. A man does not have a will that wills; he wills, with his will. The faculty is natural; the act of willing belongs to the hypostasis who exercises it.

Now apply that to the incarnate Word. He has the divine faculty, and He has the human faculty. But in every act He is one willer, and nothing He wills as man is ever willed apart from His divinity. His healing of the leper is willed divinely and humanly at once: one "I am willing," one touch of a human hand, one cleansing by divine power. The act is one, and the one who acts is one. That is what the Oriental Orthodox mean by one will: not one faculty, but one composite willing of the one incarnate Son.

Why the Trinity Analogy Cuts Both Ways

In the Trinity the rule is: one nature, one will, three persons. Notice that the rule there protects unity. It forbids three wills in God because three wills would mean three gods. The same instinct, applied to Christ, should make us cautious about multiplying the will of the one Son. If a second will in the Trinity would imply a second god, a second will in Christ, counted as a separate act, invites the suspicion of a second Son. The Oriental Orthodox simply refuse to give the suspicion any room.

This is not a denial of the human faculty. It is a refusal to let the numbering of faculties become the numbering of willers, which is where every Nestorian Christology finally ends up.

Both Families, 1990 · Paragraph 5
"He Who wills and acts is always the one Hypostasis of the Logos incarnate."

That sentence, signed by Eastern and Oriental Orthodox theologians together, is the whole Oriental Orthodox case in fourteen words. Section XIV returns to it.

Fr. Peter Farrington, an Oriental Orthodox priest and writer in the United Kingdom, draws a distinction that does more than any other to clear up the controversy. It is the difference between a movement of the faculty of will and a choice made by the one who wills.

A man fasting is hungry. His faculty of will is genuinely moved toward food; the movement is natural, involuntary and blameless. He nevertheless chooses not to eat. There were two movements in him, the pull toward food and the resolve to keep the fast, but there was only one choice, and it was his. Nobody would say he had two wills in conflict. He had one will, moved in more than one way, and he decided.

So it is with the Lord. In Gethsemane His human faculty of will is truly moved by the blameless and natural shrinking of human nature from death. That movement is real; if it were not, He would not be truly man and the agony would be theatre. But the movement is not a choice. The choice is "not as I will, but as You will," and it is the one choice of the one Son, made humanly, in perfect accord with the will He shares with the Father.

Why This Matters

If every movement of the faculty counted as a separate will, then every hungry fasting monk would have two wills, and every martyr who feared the sword and went to it anyway would have two wills at war. Counting movements as wills proves too much. What the Fathers protect is the reality of the human movement and the unity of the one who chooses. The Oriental Orthodox protect both, and they put the number on the chooser.

St. Cyril of Alexandria
On the Lord's fast in the wilderness, as summarised by Fr. Peter Farrington
The Word allowed His own flesh to feel hunger only when He willed it, at the end of the forty days, permitting the natural human desire for food to move Him when the time was right.
Honoured by BothPARAPHRASE
St. Cyril of Alexandria
Defence of the Tenth Anathema against Theodoret, as quoted by Severus in Letter I
"He wept in human fashion, that he might take away your weeping. He feared by dispensation, inasmuch as he sometimes permitted his flesh to undergo the passions proper to it, that he might make us valiant."
Honoured by BothVERBATIM · BROOKS TRANSLATION (1915)

Cyril's point is that the natural movements of the Lord's humanity were truly His and arose as and when He willed within the economy. The humanity is fully human, and it is fully His, which is exactly the Oriental Orthodox claim.

Every online debate about the will of Christ, as Fr. Peter observes, ends up in the garden counting wills. It is worth going there carefully, because both families read the passage more closely than the debates suggest.

Matthew 26:39
"O My Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from Me; nevertheless, not as I will, but as You will."
🔵 Miaphysite reading: The one Son speaks to His Father. The shrinking from the cup is the real, blameless movement of the human faculty, which He made His own; "as You will" is His one choice, made as man, identical in content with the will He shares eternally with the Father.
🟣 Dyophysite reading: "As I will" refers to Christ's natural human will, which shrinks from death; "as You will" refers to the divine will, which the Son shares with the Father. The verse shows two natural wills in perfect concord.
John 6:38
"For I have come down from heaven, not to do My own will, but the will of Him who sent Me."
🔵 Miaphysite reading: The one who came down from heaven is the subject who speaks. The Sixth Council itself reads "My own will" here as the will of His flesh, called His own because the flesh is His own. Even while counting two wills, the council insists that the human will is His own, which is a confession of the one willer.
Matthew 8:3
"Then Jesus put out His hand and touched him, saying, 'I am willing; be cleansed.'"
🔵 Miaphysite reading: The clearest image of the one theandric act. A human hand, a human voice, a divine cleansing, and a single "I am willing."
Hebrews 5:7-8
"... who, in the days of His flesh, when He had offered up prayers and supplications, with vehement cries and tears... though He was a Son, yet He learned obedience by the things which He suffered."
🔵 Miaphysite reading: Obedience learned in suffering requires a real human will, and the Oriental Orthodox affirm it gladly. But the subject is "a Son": the one who obeys is the eternal Son in the days of His flesh.
Philippians 2:8
"He humbled Himself and became obedient to the point of death, even the death of the cross."
🔵 Miaphysite reading: The one who "humbled Himself" is the one who was in the form of God. The humbling and the obedience are acts of the same subject, which is why the Church may say that the Word Himself obeyed unto death in His own humanity.
Hebrews 10:7
"Then I said, 'Behold, I have come ... to do Your will, O God.'"
🔵 Miaphysite reading: The Son's coming is itself an act of His will. The incarnation does not introduce a second willer into His life; it gives His one willing a human voice and a human body in which to be carried out.

The Point Both Sides Concede

Neither family reads Gethsemane as a conflict. Maximus himself presents it as a perfect concurrence, the human will wholly deified in its agreement with the divine. The Oriental Orthodox agree entirely. The only question left is whether to describe that perfect concurrence as two wills agreeing or as one will of the incarnate Word, humanly and divinely exercised. Given that the subject is one and the agreement is total, the Oriental Orthodox description is at least as faithful to the text, and it carries no risk of a second subject.

The Fathers did not read Gethsemane in a vacuum. They read it under a rule, and the rule was laid down by St. Cyril at Ephesus. Before looking at what each Father says about each text, it is worth seeing the rule they were all, in one way or another, trying to keep.

St. Cyril of Alexandria
Third Letter to Nestorius, Anathema IV (AD 430), received at Ephesus
"If anyone shall divide between two persons or subsistences those expressions which are contained in the Evangelical and Apostolical writings, or which have been said concerning Christ by the Saints, or by himself, and shall apply some to him as to a man separate from the Word of God, and shall apply others to the only Word of God the Father, on the ground that they are fit to be applied to God: let him be anathema."
Honoured by BothVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

This is the exegetical key. The Fathers freely say that some sayings of Christ show His divinity and others His humanity; that is distinction, and it is permitted. What Cyril forbids is distributing the sayings between two subjects. Every word in the Gospels is spoken by the one incarnate Word. Hold that rule in mind through each text below: the question is never whether Gethsemane is spoken humanly (it plainly is) but whether it is spoken by someone other than the Word (it is not).

Matthew 26:39 · "Let this cup pass from Me"

St. Athanasius the Apostolic
Against the Arians III.57 (c. AD 356)
"For He willed what He deprecated, for therefore had He come; but His was the willing (for for it He came), but the terror belonged to the flesh. Wherefore as man He utters this speech also, and yet both were said by the Same, to show that He was God, willing in Himself, but when He had become man, having a flesh that was in terror. For the sake of this flesh He combined His own will with human weakness, that destroying this affection He might in turn make man undaunted in face of death."
Honoured by BothVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Athanasius gives two things the Oriental Orthodox hold together: both sayings in the garden "were said by the Same," and the Lord "combined His own will with human weakness." He places the willing in the Word and the terror in the flesh, and refuses to give either to anyone but the one Lord.

St. John Chrysostom
Homilies on Matthew, Homily 83 (c. AD 390)
"By saying then, If it be possible, let it pass from me, He showed His humanity; but by saying, Nevertheless not as I will, but as You will, He showed His virtue and self-command, teaching us even when nature pulls us back, to follow God."
Honoured by BothVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION
St. Cyril of Alexandria
Commentary on Luke, Sermon 146 (on Luke 22:39–46)
Grief and distress cannot belong to the divine and impassible nature of the Word, which transcends all passion; but the incarnate Word willed to submit Himself to the measure of human nature. As He is said to have hungered and been weary though He is Life and the Lord of powers, so He is said to have been grieved, for it would not have befitted Him who emptied Himself to seem unwilling to endure human things.
Honoured by BothPARAPHRASE
St. Ambrose of Milan
On the Christian Faith II.7.53 (AD 378)
"My will, therefore, He took to Himself, my grief. In confidence I call it grief, because I preach His Cross. Mine is the will which He called His own, for as man He bore my grief, as man He spoke, and therefore said, Not as I will, but as You will."
Honoured by BothVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Ambrose is the clearest Latin witness for the human will in Gethsemane, and he says just before this that "the will of God is one, and the human will another." The Oriental Orthodox have no quarrel with the distinction. Note that Ambrose's whole point is that this human will is the one He called His own.

What the witnesses show. Every one of these Fathers attributes the shrinking from death to the humanity, and every one attributes it to the one Lord. Ambrose and Chrysostom distinguish the human and divine sharply, and the Oriental Orthodox need not deny that the two can be distinguished in the verse. What none of them does is place a second subject in the garden. Athanasius and Cyril, the two Fathers the Oriental Orthodox follow most closely, both read the scene as the one Word allowing His own humanity to feel what is natural to it, and then triumphing in it.

John 6:38 · "Not to do My own will"

St. Gregory the Theologian
Oration 30 (Fourth Theological Oration), §12 (AD 380)
Had this not been said by the very one who came down, we might take it as spoken from the human nature, for in the Saviour the human will cannot oppose God, being altogether taken into God, though in us it often resists. But since it is He who came down who says it, the meaning is that the Son has no will of His own apart from the will common to Him and the Father, as They have one Godhead.
Honoured by BothPARAPHRASE

The Sixth Council quotes this very passage. Note what Gregory does. He grants that the human will in Christ is real and wholly taken into God, but he refuses to read the saying as the voice of the human nature on its own, because the speaker is the one who came down from heaven. The subject determines the reading. That is the Oriental Orthodox emphasis exactly.

John 12:27 · "Now My soul is troubled"

St. Cyril of Alexandria
Commentary on John, Book VIII (on John 12:27)
"Profitably were the human feelings troubled in Christ: not that the emotions should prevail and go forward, as in us; but that, having begun, they might be cut short by the power of the Word, nature in Christ first being transelemented into some better and Diviner condition. For in this way and no other was it that the process of the healing passed over even unto us."
Honoured by BothVERBATIM · LFC TRANSLATION (1874)

Cyril adds in the same place that the trouble shows "He indeed had a rational soul." The fear is natural, sinless and truly His; it begins, and it is cut short by the power of the Word, and our nature is healed in His.

This is Cyril's characteristic pattern, and it is the patristic root of the movement and choice distinction in Section VI. The faculty is truly moved; the Word does not let the movement become the deciding act. One subject, real passion, one victory.

Luke 22:43–44 · The Agony and the Bloody Sweat

St. Justin Martyr
Dialogue with Trypho 99 and 103 (c. AD 155)
"He prayed in these words: 'Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me.' And again He prayed: 'Not as I will, but as You will.' showing by this that He had become truly a suffering man."
Honoured by BothVERBATIM · ANF TRANSLATION

In chapter 103 Justin adds, from the apostles' memoirs, that "His sweat fell down like drops of blood while He was praying," and that this happened "in order that we may perceive that the Father wished His Son really to undergo such sufferings for our sakes." One of the earliest Christian writers on the garden reads it as proof that the Son truly suffered as man.

St. Irenaeus of Lyons
Against Heresies III.22.2 (c. AD 180)
If He had not truly taken flesh from Mary, He would not have hungered, nor wearied, nor wept over Lazarus, nor sweated great drops of blood, nor said that His soul was sorrowful. These are the tokens of the flesh He took from the earth, which He took up into Himself, bringing salvation to His own handiwork.
Honoured by BothPARAPHRASE

Irenaeus, writing against the Gnostics, uses the agony to prove that Christ's humanity was real. Together with Justin Martyr before him, this is among the earliest uses of Gethsemane in Christian theology, and it is the use the Oriental Orthodox have always made of it: proof of a true and complete humanity, not proof of a second person.

Hebrews 5:7–8 · "He learned obedience"

St. Cyril of Alexandria
That the Christ is One (On the Unity of Christ)
"Hence the Word of God no longer bare and imparticipate in the measures of the emptying but in the days of His flesh has been made a pattern to us; in that then without any blame He could use the measures of the human nature and prolong His prayer and shed the tear and seem now both to need a Saviour and to learn obedience, albeit Son."
Honoured by BothVERBATIM · PUSEY TRANSLATION (1881)

Cyril reads Hebrews 5 as the Word Himself, now no longer "bare" of our condition, using "the measures of the human nature" to become a pattern for us in prayer, tears and obedience. The one who learns obedience is the Son, "albeit Son."

Mark 13:32 · "Of that day and hour no one knows"

St. Athanasius the Apostolic
Against the Arians III.42–46
The Son says He knows not the day as man, for ignorance belongs to the flesh; but the same Son, through whom all times and seasons were made, knows the day as the Word. Athanasius does not assign the ignorance to another subject but to the one Lord according to His humanity.
Honoured by BothPARAPHRASE

The passage is not about will, but it shows the method. The same pattern governs knowledge, suffering and willing: distinguish what belongs to the humanity, never divide the one to whom it belongs.

The Fathers below are arranged in order of time. The first group wrote before Chalcedon and belongs to both Orthodox families equally. The second group are the Oriental Orthodox Fathers after 451. The third group, for fairness, are the Chalcedonian Fathers on whom the doctrine of two wills was built. Each card is labelled with the tradition that venerates the writer.

Before Chalcedon: The Common Fathers

St. Ignatius of Antioch
Letter to the Ephesians 7 (c. AD 107)
"There is one Physician who is possessed both of flesh and spirit; both made and not made; God existing in flesh; true life in death; both of Mary and of God; first passible and then impassible, even Jesus Christ our Lord."
Honoured by BothVERBATIM · ANF TRANSLATION

Early in the second century, Ignatius piles up the paradoxes on a single subject: one Physician, both passible and impassible. The Oriental Orthodox insistence on the one who wills grows from this root.

St. Irenaeus of Lyons
Against Heresies III.19.3 (c. AD 180)
As He became man to undergo temptation, so He was the Word that He might be glorified. The Word remained quiescent so that He could be tempted, dishonoured, crucified and suffer death, while the human nature was swallowed up in the divine when it conquered, endured, rose and was taken up.
Honoured by BothPARAPHRASE

Irenaeus distinguishes the human and divine aspects of a single career. Notice that he does not need two wills to do it. He needs one Word who holds back His power so that His own humanity can be tempted and suffer, and in whom that humanity then conquers. The subject of the temptation and the subject of the victory are the same.

St. Hippolytus of Rome
Against the Heresy of Noetus 18 (c. AD 200)
"He hungers and toils and thirsts in weariness, and flees in fear, and prays in trouble. And He who as God has a sleepless nature, slumbers on a pillow. And He who for this end came into the world, begs off from the cup of suffering. And in an agony He sweats blood, and is strengthened by an angel, who Himself strengthens those who believe in Him."
Honoured by BothVERBATIM · ANF TRANSLATION

Hippolytus, around AD 200, already reads the cup, the bloody sweat and the angel as the acts of the one "who for this end came into the world." The same subject begs off the cup and came to drink it, and the one strengthened by the angel is the one who strengthens believers.

St. Athanasius the Apostolic
On the Incarnation 18 (c. AD 318)
"But these things are said of Him, because the actual body which ate, was born, and suffered, belonged to none other but to the Lord: and because, having become man, it was proper for these things to be predicated of Him as man, to show Him to have a body in truth, and not in seeming."
Honoured by BothVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

In the chapter before, Athanasius insists that the Word was not circumscribed by the body and never ceased ordering the universe. Here he adds the other half: what the body did and suffered belonged "to none other but to the Lord." The 20th Pope of Alexandria gives the model the Oriental Orthodox apply to the will. There is no separate agent in the flesh.

St. Basil the Great
Letter 261 (c. AD 377)
There are natural affections and there are affections that come from wickedness. The Lord took the natural ones, such as weariness, pain, hunger, thirst, grief and anxiety, to show that His incarnation was real and not an appearance; but He did not take those that defile, for He was without sin.
Honoured by BothPARAPHRASE

Basil supplies the vocabulary of blameless passion that the Oriental Orthodox use for Gethsemane. The shrinking from death in Gethsemane belongs in Basil's first category: natural, sinless, and truly taken. It is a movement of His humanity, not a rival choice.

St. Gregory the Theologian
Letter 101, to Cledonius (c. AD 382)
"For that which He has not assumed He has not healed; but that which is united to His Godhead is also saved."
Honoured by BothVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

This line is often thrown at the Oriental Orthodox as if it proved two wills. It proves a complete humanity, mind and will included, which the Oriental Orthodox confess without reservation. And note its second half: what is healed is what is united to the Godhead. Gregory's emphasis falls on union.

St. Gregory of Nyssa
Catechetical Oration 9–10 (c. AD 385)
Nothing in human nature degrades God except evil, so the incarnation is not unworthy of Him. Nor was the infinite enclosed in the flesh as in a vessel: the divine is united to the human as a flame clings to the wick, truly present and active in it without being contained by it.
Honoured by BothPARAPHRASE

Ephrem's hymns marvel again and again that the one who fills all things dwelt in the womb, that the one who feeds creation was nursed as an infant. The Syriac poet does not tire of saying that it is the same one who does the divine and the human things.

After Chalcedon: The Oriental Orthodox Fathers

St. Dioscorus of Alexandria
Statements reported in the Syriac tradition (AD 451–454)
Dioscorus anathematised those who assign two properties and two activities to Christ, which he took to be the teaching of Leo's Tome, and insisted that the divinity of his Lord was not separated from the humanity for a single moment, not even in death.
Oriental Orthodox SaintPARAPHRASE

The 25th Pope of Alexandria died in exile for refusing Chalcedon. His concern, from the first, was division, and every Oriental Orthodox statement on the will inherits it.

St. Timothy II of Alexandria
Letters from exile and writings against Chalcedon (c. AD 460–475)
Timothy, called Aelurus, condemns Eutyches by name for denying that Christ's flesh is consubstantial with ours, while also rejecting the Tome of Leo for appearing to give each nature its own acts as if they were two agents. He holds both lines at once: no confusion, no division.
Oriental Orthodox SaintPARAPHRASE

Timothy matters here because he shows that the Oriental Orthodox fought on two fronts from the start. Those who deny Christ a real human nature, and with it a real human will, were condemned by the Oriental Orthodox themselves.

St. Philoxenus of Mabbug
Discourses and Letters (c. AD 485–523)
Philoxenus is the great defender of the confession that one of the Holy Trinity became man without change and was crucified for us. Everything Christ did and suffered, he insists, must be confessed of that one, the eternal Son, and not shared out between God and a man.
Oriental Orthodox SaintPARAPHRASE

This is the Oriental Orthodox answer to the passage in Leo's Tome which assigns weeping over a dead friend to one form and raising him with a word of command to the other. The one who wept at the tomb is the one who called Lazarus out of it.

St. Severus of Antioch
Letters to Sergius the Grammarian (early 6th century)
Against Sergius, who wanted to speak of a single property of Christ after the union and so blurred the difference between Godhead and flesh, Severus insists that the natures from which Christ is remain each in its own natural quality after the union, without change. The flesh remains flesh and the soul remains a rational soul. Composition does not abolish what is composed.
Oriental Orthodox SaintPARAPHRASE

This exchange is the clearest proof that the Oriental Orthodox never held the confusion of natures their opponents attributed to them. Sergius the Grammarian argued for one property that blurred divinity and humanity, and Severus, the greatest Oriental Orthodox theologian, wrote at length to refute him.

St. Jacob of Serugh
Homilies (Memre) on the Passion (c. AD 500–521)
Jacob's homilies on the Passion set the Creator and the Crucified side by side in line after line, insisting that it was the Son of God Himself, and no other, who thirsted, was mocked and died. The humble act and the divine identity are always bound together in one subject.
Oriental Orthodox SaintPARAPHRASE
St. Dionysius the Areopagite
Letter IV, to Gaius (c. AD 500)
"He was not man, not as not being man, but as being from men was beyond men, and was above man, having truly been born man."

The same letter ends with the "new God-incarnate energy" discussed in Section X. Here Dionysius holds both sides together: Christ is truly man, and yet as man He is beyond men.

St. Nerses IV Shnorhali
Letters to the Emperor Manuel Comnenus (c. 1165–1173)
In his correspondence with the Byzantine court, the Armenian Catholicos argued that since both Churches received the Council of Ephesus and the teaching of St. Cyril, there was no clear reason for them to remain divided, and he worked patiently for a union that would respect each Church's way of speaking.
Oriental Orthodox SaintPARAPHRASE

Eight centuries before the modern dialogues, an Oriental Orthodox Catholicos was already treating the Christological divide as a matter for patient reconciliation on the common ground of Cyril.

The Chalcedonian Fathers, Stated Fairly

An honest page gives the other side in its own best form. These are the Fathers on whom the doctrine of two wills was built.

Pope Leo I of Rome
The Tome, to Flavian (AD 449)
"For each form does what is proper to it with the co-operation of the other; that is the Word performing what appertains to the Word, and the flesh carrying out what appertains to the flesh."
ChalcedonianVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

This is the sentence the Oriental Orthodox objected to from the start. Read charitably, "with the co-operation of the other" protects the unity. Read as Dioscorus and Timothy read it, "each form does" sounds like two agents. Every later argument about the will is an argument about how to read this sentence.

St. Sophronius of Jerusalem
Synodical Letter (AD 634)
The one and the same Christ works both the divine and the human things, each nature acting with the communion of the other, so that He does not do divine things divinely apart from the flesh, nor human things humanly apart from the divinity.
ChalcedonianPARAPHRASE

Sophronius, the first great opponent of monoenergism, insists as firmly as any Oriental Orthodox writer that one and the same Christ does both the divine and the human things. On that point the Oriental Orthodox can read him with real sympathy.

St. Maximus the Confessor
Opuscule 6, on the Gethsemane prayer (c. AD 640s)
In the agony the Lord shows that He has a natural human will, which shrinks from death, and shows that this will is wholly in agreement with the divine will He shares with the Father, giving us the pattern of setting aside our own will to fulfil God's.
ChalcedonianPARAPHRASE
St. John of Damascus
Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith III.18 (c. AD 740)
"So that the soul of the Lord being moved of its own free-will willed, but willed of its free-will those things which His divine will willed it to will... But He Himself, Who was one and yet both God and man, willed according to both His divine and His human will."
ChalcedonianVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Notice the Damascene's subject: He Himself, Who was one. That is the 1990 agreed statement's paragraph 5, written in the eighth century by the great systematiser of Chalcedonian theology. In fairness it must be added that in III.14 he argues directly against speaking of one compound will, as Maximus did. The Oriental Orthodox put the emphasis on the one who wills and decline to count; the Damascene puts it on the two natures and counts.

A Modern Witness

Pope Shenouda III of Alexandria
The Nature of Christ (Coptic Orthodox Patriarchate)
Because after the union there is one nature of the incarnate Word, the Coptic Church also confesses one will and one act. Yet what the divine nature wills is the same as what the human nature wills, since there is no contradiction or conflict whatever between them.
Coptic PatriarchPARAPHRASE

The 117th Pope of Alexandria states the position for Coptic catechesis in the twentieth century. Note that he speaks of what the human nature wills: the human will is presupposed even in the most compact statement of the one will.

What the Fathers Add Up To

From Ignatius to Shenouda, three things hold constant. Christ's humanity is complete, with its own natural affections and its own will. Everything He does, humanly and divinely, is done by the one Word. And the human and divine are distinguished without being divided. The Chalcedonian Fathers add a fourth, that the natural wills are to be counted as two. The Oriental Orthodox Fathers decline that fourth step, not because they deny what is counted, but because they will not let counting suggest a second Son.

The controversy began with energy before it reached will, and the Oriental Orthodox vocabulary here is not new. It comes from a Father both families honour.

St. Dionysius the Areopagite
Letter IV, to Gaius (c. AD 500)
"Not having done things Divine as God, nor things human as man, but exercising for us a certain new God-incarnate energy of God having become man."
Honoured by BothVERBATIM · PARKER TRANSLATION (1897)

The Greek word Parker renders "God-incarnate" is theandrikē, divine-human. Dionysius speaks of an energy, singular, and calls it new. Severus seized on the phrase, and with good reason: it describes exactly what the Oriental Orthodox mean. Not a third energy that is neither divine nor human, and not the divine energy alone overriding the human, but the one composite activity of the incarnate Word in which both remain what they are.

St. Cyril of Alexandria
Commentary on John, Book IV (on John 6:53)
"When He was raising the little daughter of the chief of the Synagogue saying, Maid, arise, He laid hold of her hand, as it is written, giving life, as God, by His All-Powerful command, and again, giving life through the touch of His Holy Flesh, He shews that there was one kindred operation through both."
Honoured by BothVERBATIM · LFC TRANSLATION (1874)

Cyril chooses the raising of Jairus' daughter and the widow's son at Nain: a divine command and a human touch, and the life that results. The energy is one through both. Maximus quotes this passage and argues that Cyril called the energy one by reason of the union, not as one natural energy. Perhaps so. But if Cyril may say one energy without confusing the natures, so may Severus, and so may the Oriental Orthodox. The same charity should be extended to all three.

St. Severus of Antioch
Letter I, to Oecumenius the Count, on Properties and Operations (c. AD 508–512)
"Some of them are acts befitting the divinity, while others are human. For example, to walk and travel in bodily form upon the earth is without contention human; but to bestow on those who are maimed in the feet and cannot walk upon the ground at all the power of walking like sound persons is God-befitting. Yet the one Word incarnate performed the latter and the former, and the one nature did not perform the one, and the other the other."
Oriental Orthodox SaintVERBATIM · BROOKS TRANSLATION (1915)

Here is the Oriental Orthodox doctrine of operation in Severus' own words. The acts are distinguished, human and divine, and Severus insists on the difference. But the agent is one: "the one Word incarnate performed the latter and the former." In the same letter he quotes the Tome's sentence that each form "effects in partnership with the other that which belongs to itself," and rejects it for this reason.

Severus (died 538) wrote a full century before the monothelite controversy, so he never addresses its questions directly. What he does say is more interesting than either side usually admits, and an honest page must report all of it.

Severus Speaks of Two Wills

In one passage Severus says that the one Christ, one from two and one incarnate nature of the Word, "also displays two wills in salvific suffering," the one that requests and the one that is prepared, the one human and the other divine. This is sometimes quoted by Chalcedonians to embarrass the Oriental Orthodox. It should instead be quoted by the Oriental Orthodox, because it shows that the distinction of divine and human willing was never foreign to their greatest theologian.

St. Severus of Antioch
Letter I, to Oecumenius the Count (c. AD 508–512)
"Flesh does not renounce its existence as flesh, even if it has become God's flesh, nor has the Word departed from his nature, even if he has been hypostatically united to flesh which possesses a rational and intelligent soul: but the difference also is preserved... they recognise the difference only, not admitting a division: for the principle of union does not admit of division."
Oriental Orthodox SaintVERBATIM · BROOKS TRANSLATION (1915)

Written before he became patriarch, this letter shows what Severus meant: a flesh with "a rational and intelligent soul," and therefore with a human will, whose difference from the Godhead "is preserved." What he refuses is division, never difference.

Severus Refuses to Divide Them

Elsewhere, in a passage cited by Cyril Hovorun in Will, Action and Freedom, Severus reads St. Athanasius on the Lord's prayer to be spared suffering. He says that Athanasius rightly calls the request a will, which shows that the fear and trembling were taken on by Christ voluntarily: a will was truly present, and the suffering was not involuntary. But he adds that Athanasius does not divide into two wills what belongs to one and the same incarnate God, since the one who suffers in weakness lives by the power of God.

Put the two passages side by side and the Oriental Orthodox position appears exactly. Severus distinguishes divine and human willing in contemplation, in the way that the human request and the divine readiness can be told apart in Gethsemane. He refuses to separate them in reality, because both belong to the one incarnate Word. Distinction without division: that is Severus on the natures, and it is Severus on the will.

St. Severus of Antioch
As cited by Fr. Peter Farrington, "The Orthodox Understanding of the Will of Christ" (2017)
The one Christ is from two, from divinity and humanity, one person and hypostasis, the one incarnate nature of the Word become perfect man. For this reason He also displays two wills in salvific suffering: the one which requests and the other which is prepared, the one human and the other divine.
Oriental Orthodox SaintPARAPHRASE

Note Severus' reasoning: it is because Christ is one from two that He displays both wills. The two wills are a consequence of the composition, not a rival to it.

On St. Dioscorus

Grillmeier and others cite St. Dioscorus anathematising those who assume two properties and two activities in Christ. Read in context, Dioscorus is attacking the Tome of Leo as he understood it, a Tome he believed separated the acts of Christ between two subjects. He is condemning division, not affirming that Christ lacks human activity. The same Dioscorus insisted that the divinity was not separated from the humanity even for a moment, which presupposes a real and complete humanity to be united.

This is the section that most needs correcting, because the popular version has the parties reversed. Monothelitism was not an Oriental Orthodox doctrine that a council rejected. It was a Chalcedonian doctrine, devised by Chalcedonians, to bring the Oriental Orthodox back into communion with the imperial Church.

St. Sophronius of Jerusalem, a Chalcedonian, protests against the formula. The quarrel is now inside the Chalcedonian Church.
AD 638
The Ekthesis of Heraclius, drafted by Sergius, forbids talk of energies and proclaims one will in Christ.
AD 639–642
The Arab conquest of Egypt ends imperial pressure on the Copts. Pope Benjamin returns in 644. The union collapses.

What the History Shows

The people condemned in 681 were not Copts, Syriacs, Armenians or Ethiopians. They were the bishops of Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch and Rome in communion with Chalcedon. The doctrine they were condemned for was two natures with one will, a formula the Oriental Orthodox never held, since they did not accept the two natures formula on which it was built.

The Sixth Council did not ignore the Oriental Orthodox. Its definition recalls Chalcedon's judgment against "Eutyches and Dioscorus," and it describes monothelitism as a heresy similar to "the mad and wicked doctrine of the impious Apollinaris, Severus, and Themistius." The council regarded the Oriental Orthodox as already outside the Church, and it believed their teaching resembled the heresy it was condemning.

But notice what this does and does not say. The council did not condemn the Oriental Orthodox for monothelitism: they were not present, were not party to the dispute, and every one of the men anathematised for the doctrine was a Chalcedonian. What it did was draw a comparison, and the comparison rests on an assumption, namely that Severus denied the human will its own natural motion. Severus' own writings contradict it. He made the human choice of the good the very means of our healing (Section IV), he insisted that Christ's flesh has "a rational and intelligent soul" whose difference from the Godhead is preserved (Section XI), and he wrote at length against Sergius the Grammarian for confusing the natures (Section IX). The comparison was polemic, made nearly a century and a half after Severus' death.

None of this is to dishonour St. Maximus or St. Sophronius, whose courage was real. Maximus suffered for refusing an emperor's theology, and the Copts know what that costs. It is to insist that the heresy he fought was not ours.

Fr. Peter Farrington, having read Maximus in Fr. Andrew Louth's translation, concludes that much of what Maximus says is immediately acceptable to an Oriental Orthodox Christology, and that his real objection is to the way Maximus characterises his opponents rather than to the substance of his teaching. That is worth taking seriously, because it means the gap is narrower than either side's polemicists suppose.

Where Maximus Is Right

Maximus insists that a rational soul necessarily has a natural will; that Christ has no gnomic, wavering will; that there is no contrariety between the human and divine in Him; and that the human will is divine by union and not by nature. The Oriental Orthodox affirm all four without reservation.

St. Maximus the Confessor
Opuscule 7, in Andrew Louth, Maximus the Confessor (Routledge, 1996)
The human will is wholly deified in its agreement with the divine will, being eternally moved and shaped by it. It can properly be called truly divine in virtue of the union, though not by nature.
ChalcedonianPARAPHRASE

Where Maximus Misreads Us

Maximus argues that a composite will would be something foreign to both the Father and to us, since neither the Father nor we have a composite will. But this attacks a position no Oriental Orthodox Father held. The composite will is not a third kind of will produced by melting two into one. It names the union in which the divine will, consubstantial with the Father, and the human will, consubstantial with us, are both fully present as the will of the one Word. Maximus allows Dionysius to speak of one theandric energy and Cyril to speak of one energy through both, explaining each charitably. The same charity was owed to Severus.

The Sixth Council's Own Words

The definition of 681 is closer to the Oriental Orthodox position than most people realise. Read it slowly.

Third Council of Constantinople
Definition of Faith (AD 681)
"His human will follows and that not as resisting and reluctant, but rather as subject to his divine and omnipotent will... For as his flesh is called and is the flesh of God the Word, so also the natural will of his flesh is called and is the proper will of God the Word."
ChalcedonianVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

The human will is the proper will of God the Word. It is His own, just as His flesh is His own. It follows, it does not resist, it is subject. Almost everything the Oriental Orthodox mean by one composite will is contained in that sentence except the decision to count. The council counts two because it starts from the natures; the Oriental Orthodox say one because they start from the Word whose will it is. In the words of the 1990 statement, both families have kept the same Orthodox faith while using Christological terms in different ways.

The Joint Commission of the Theological Dialogue between the Orthodox Church and the Oriental Orthodox Churches met at the Monastery of St. Bishoy in Egypt in 1989 and at Chambésy in Switzerland in 1990. The Second Agreed Statement addresses the will of Christ directly, and its wording is worth knowing well.

1990 · Paragraph 3
Both families agree that the Hypostasis of the Logos became composite by uniting to His divine uncreated nature, with its natural will and energy shared with the Father and the Spirit, a created human nature with its own natural will and energy, which He assumed and made His own. (Paraphrased)
1990 · Paragraph 4
Both families agree that the natures, with their proper energies and wills, are united hypostatically and naturally, without confusion, change, division or separation, and distinguished in thought alone. (Paraphrased)
1990 · Paragraph 5
"He Who wills and acts is always the one Hypostasis of the Logos incarnate."

Why These Paragraphs Matter

Three things follow. First, both sides affirmed that the hypostasis of the Logos became composite. Chalcedonian theology already speaks this way, in the Fifth Council's language of synthetic union and in writers such as Maximus and John of Damascus; what Maximus rejected was a composite will, and the agreed language of composition shows how close that question lies to common ground. Second, the Oriental Orthodox signatories agreed that each nature has its proper will and energy, which disposes of the charge that they deny a human will. Third, both agreed that the one who wills is always one. Read together, the paragraphs say what the Oriental Orthodox have always meant by one will of the incarnate Word, and what the Chalcedonians have always meant by two natural wills in one hypostasis.

The statements have not been formally received by every Church on either side, and they do not settle every question of terminology. But they show that the charge of monothelitism against the Oriental Orthodox cannot be maintained by anyone who has read them.

The objections below are stated as their strongest defenders would state them.

OO Response: The objection assumes that one will means one faculty, and that the one faculty is divine. The Oriental Orthodox mean neither. The human faculty of will is fully assumed, and Severus makes its free choice of the good the very means by which our wills are healed. St. Gregory the Theologian's principle is affirmed in full: the Word assumed a complete human will, and that is exactly why it is healed.

What is denied is not the human faculty but a second willer. The principle that what is not assumed is not healed was written against Apollinarius, who took the human mind away from Christ. It was never written against those who confess one Christ whose human mind and will are His own.

OO Response: The faculty of will belongs to nature; the Oriental Orthodox agree, which is why they affirm both faculties in Christ. But the act of willing belongs to the one who wills, and in Christ the one who wills is the one Son. Paragraph 5 of the 1990 statement, signed by both families, says exactly this.

Note also what the Trinitarian rule actually protects. One will in God guards against three gods. Applied to Christ, the same concern for unity should make us slow to multiply the willing of the one Son. The Oriental Orthodox do not deny two natural faculties; they decline to count the willing of the incarnate Word as two acts, because the willer is one.

OO Response: Gethsemane shows the real human shrinking from death and the perfect human submission to the Father. The Oriental Orthodox affirm both. What it does not show is two willers in Christ, and no Orthodox reading on either side finds them there.

The shrinking is a movement of the human faculty; the submission is the one choice of the one Son. A fasting man who is hungry and keeps the fast is not said to have two wills, and neither is the Lord. The passage is a dialogue between the Son and the Father, not between two parts of the Son.

OO Response: The Sixth Council anathematised Sergius, Pyrrhus, Paul, Peter, Theodore of Pharan, Cyrus, Macarius, Stephen and Pope Honorius, every one a Chalcedonian, for teaching one will on top of two natures, which the council judged to leave the humanity without its own motion. The Oriental Orthodox were not present, were not party to the dispute, and also reject the denial of the human will's natural motion.

It is true that the definition likens the heresy to the doctrine of Severus, and an honest answer must say so. But a comparison is not a demonstration. It assumes that Severus denied the human will its natural motion, and his writings show the opposite: the infant Emmanuel choosing the good in His own human soul, the long refutation of Sergius the Grammarian, the two wills he says Christ displays in His saving suffering.

Meanwhile the council's positive teaching, that the natural will of His flesh is the proper will of God the Word and follows without resistance, is something the Oriental Orthodox can affirm word for word. What remains is the question of counting, which the council settled in its own terms and the Oriental Orthodox answer in theirs.

OO Response: This was Maximus' criticism, and it rests on reading composition as fusion. The Fifth Council uses the same word, synthetic, for the union of natures and says it preserves the natures unconfused. The 1990 statement, signed by Chalcedonian theologians, calls the hypostasis of the Logos composite.

A composite will no more creates a third kind of will than one incarnate nature creates a third kind of nature. Both terms describe the union of two things that remain themselves. If Dionysius may speak of a new theandric energy without producing a hybrid, so may Severus.

OO Response: Real human obedience requires a real human faculty of will freely choosing the Father's will, and the Oriental Orthodox affirm it. Severus' whole account of salvation depends on it. What real obedience does not require is a second person doing the obeying. The one who obeys unto death is the Son who was in the form of God, as Philippians 2 insists.

In fact the Oriental Orthodox account makes the obedience more profound, not less. It is not a man who obeys God on the Son's behalf. It is God the Word Himself who obeys, humanly, in His own human will.

Yes, completely. His humanity has a rational soul and therefore a natural human will, which is His own and which freely chooses the good. What the Oriental Orthodox decline to do is count His willing as two separate acts, because He who wills is always one.

No. Monothelitism was a seventh century Chalcedonian formula, judged to deny the humanity its own natural motion, and it was condemned in 681 in the persons of Chalcedonian patriarchs and a pope. The Oriental Orthodox reject the denial of the human will's natural motion just as firmly.

Anathema & Other Churches' Baptism

"Yet do not count him as an enemy, but admonish him as a brother." 2 Thessalonians 3:15

Home›Oriental Orthodoxy›Anathema & Other Churches' Baptism
Mor Gabriel MonasterySyriac Orthodox · Tur Abdin

Many people who come close to Orthodoxy stop at this point. On one side they see the anathema. Councils end their definitions with "let him be anathema," and the word sounds like a sentence that cuts a person off from God and from salvation. On the other side they see Orthodox churches today receiving Christians from other churches without baptizing them again, signing joint declarations with their leaders and calling them brothers. If the first is meant seriously, how can the second be right? If the second is right, was the first only rhetoric?

The harshness is not the difficulty this page is about. Scripture is harsh in places, and the Fathers did not think false teaching was harmless. The difficulty is consistency. So the page sets out the evidence in order: what the word means in Scripture, how the councils used it, how the early Church received people from separated groups, how the Oriental Orthodox Fathers received converts from Chalcedon after 451, and what the churches do today. Section IX gives a verdict, including the places where real tension remains.

The short answer: the anathema and the acceptance of a baptism given outside the Church were never two rival policies. In the ancient rite of reception they were one act. A convert whose baptism was not repeated was received precisely by anathematizing the error he was leaving. What has always varied is the judgment about which groups had kept enough of the faith and of the form of baptism for that gentler way to be used. That variation is old, and the Fathers defended it. What is newer is the warmth of modern relations, some ecumenical statements that run ahead of what the churches have formally decided, and in some places a practice stricter than the ancient one.

Small misprints in the scanned 1904 edition (broken words at line ends) have been silently corrected. Where something is described rather than quoted, it carries no quotation marks and is marked SUMMARY. Scripture follows the NKJV.

Anathema
ἀνάθεμα: "something set apart"
In the Greek Old Testament it translates the Hebrew herem, a thing devoted to God and taken out of common use, often for destruction (Leviticus 27:28). Paul uses it for "accursed." In church law it is the gravest sentence: formal separation from the communion of the Church, pronounced on a teaching and on those who hold it.
Excommunication
ἀφορισμός, aphorismos: "marking off"
Exclusion from Holy Communion, usually for a set time as a penance. The canons treat it as lighter than anathema, and a person under it is still a member of the Church under discipline.
Akribeia
ἀκρίβεια: "exactness"
Applying the canons in their full strictness.
Economy
οἰκονομία, oikonomia: "management of a household"
A bishop's pastoral judgment to apply a rule more gently for the salvation of souls. It may change how a rule is applied. It never changes the faith.
Heresy, Schism, Unlawful Assembly
St Basil's three categories
Heresy is a break over the faith itself. Schism is a break over church questions that could be settled. An unlawful assembly is a group gathered by disorderly clergy or laymen. The Fathers received each differently (Section V).
The Three Ways of Reception
baptism, chrismation, confession
A convert may be received by baptism, by chrismation (anointing with the holy myron), or by a confession of the Orthodox faith with a written renunciation and anathema of the error. Which is used depends on what the group he comes from believed and how it baptized.
Libellus
"little book," a written statement
The signed document in which a convert renounced his former error and confessed the Orthodox faith. The ancient canons call it a "written renunciation" or "certificate."
Communion
κοινωνία, koinonia
Sharing in the one Eucharist, which presupposes one faith. Recognizing that a person was baptized is not the same as being in communion with him.

In the New Testament the word is rare but heavy. Paul uses it a handful of times (Romans 9:3; 1 Corinthians 12:3; 16:22; Galatians 1:8–9). Three of these show what it means.

Galatians 1:8–9
"But even if we, or an angel from heaven, preach any other gospel to you than what we have preached to you, let him be accursed. As we have said before, so now I say again, if anyone preaches any other gospel to you than what you have received, let him be accursed."
Orthodox reading: the anathema guards the gospel itself. Paul includes himself and even an angel: the sentence attaches to the false gospel, whoever preaches it. This is the pattern the councils follow.
1 Corinthians 16:22
"If anyone does not love the Lord Jesus Christ, let him be accursed. O Lord, come!"
Orthodox reading: the line between belonging and not belonging is drawn around Christ Himself.
Romans 9:3
"For I could wish that I myself were accursed from Christ for my brethren, my countrymen according to the flesh,"
Orthodox reading: "accursed from Christ" means separation from Christ, the worst thing Paul can imagine. He names it only as the measure of his love for Israel. Scripture does not treat the word lightly, and neither should we.

Beside these, the New Testament describes how the Church separates a member, and every passage joins the severity to a purpose.

1 Corinthians 5:4–5
"In the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, when you are gathered together, along with my spirit, with the power of our Lord Jesus Christ, deliver such a one to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, that his spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord Jesus."
Orthodox reading: the most severe act in the Church's hands is still aimed at salvation "in the day of the Lord Jesus."
2 Corinthians 2:6–8
"This punishment which was inflicted by the majority is sufficient for such a man, so that, on the contrary, you ought rather to forgive and comfort him, lest perhaps such a one be swallowed up with too much sorrow. Therefore I urge you to reaffirm your love to him."
Orthodox reading: the same apostle who imposed the sentence asks for it to be relaxed once it has done its work. Strictness and relaxation come from one mind.
2 Thessalonians 3:14–15
"And if anyone does not obey our word in this epistle, note that person and do not keep company with him, that he may be ashamed. Yet do not count him as an enemy, but admonish him as a brother."
Matthew 18:17–18
"And if he refuses to hear them, tell it to the church. But if he refuses even to hear the church, let him be to you like a heathen and a tax collector. Assuredly, I say to you, whatever you bind on earth will be bound in heaven, and whatever you loose on earth will be loosed in heaven."
Orthodox reading: the Lord gives the Church power both to bind and to loose. A Church that could only bind, or only loose, would not be using the authority He gave.
Titus 3:10–11
"Reject a divisive man after the first and second admonition, knowing that such a person is warped and sinning, being self-condemned."
Orthodox reading: the one rejected is the man who persists after warning, and Paul calls him "self-condemned." The Church's sentence recognizes a choice the person has already made.

Two more passages keep the picture balanced. When the disciples stopped a man who cast out demons in Christ's name "because he does not follow us," the Lord answered, "Do not forbid him, for no one who works a miracle in My name can soon afterward speak evil of Me. For he who is not against us is on our side" (Mark 9:38–40). He did not make the man one of the Twelve, but He did not deny that His name was at work. And Paul draws a line around his own authority to judge:

1 Corinthians 5:12–13
"For what have I to do with judging those also who are outside? Do you not judge those who are inside? But those who are outside God judges. Therefore “put away from yourselves the evil person.”"
Orthodox reading: the Church's discipline is for her own members. Those outside are left to the judgment of God.

The Form of a Conciliar Anathema

The councils used the word the way Paul did in Galatians: against a teaching, and against whoever holds it. The Creed of Nicaea in 325 listed the Arian claims and ended:

First Council of Nicaea
The Creed of 325
"...or that he is a creature, or subject to change or conversion... all that so say, the Catholic and Apostolic Church anathematizes them."
Ecumenical CouncilVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION
First Council of Constantinople
Canon 1 (AD 381)
"The Faith of the Three Hundred and Eighteen Fathers assembled at Nice in Bithynia shall not be set aside, but shall remain firm. And every heresy shall be anathematized..."
Ecumenical CouncilVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

St Cyril's twelve chapters against Nestorius, which the Oriental Orthodox treasure, have the same shape: "If anyone..." followed by the teaching, then "let him be anathema" (see St Cyril of Alexandria). The sentence is conditional. It describes a belief and attaches the sentence to whoever holds it.

It is only fair to add that councils also anathematized named persons: Arius, Nestorius, Eutyches. After 451 the two Orthodox families anathematized one another's councils and fathers by name. The Oriental Orthodox rejected the Council of Chalcedon and the Tome of Leo, and the Council in Trullo (692) condemned Dioscorus by name in its first canon. Those named anathemas are the ones the modern dialogue proposes to lift (Section VIII). The anathemas against the heresies themselves are not proposed for lifting by anyone.

The Anathema Is the Back of a Confession

St Athanasius explained why the Nicene Creed ends with anathemas. They are not a separate act of hostility. They are the same faith stated in the negative.

St Athanasius
De Synodis 40 (AD 359)
"...in order to show that the statements are parallel, and that the terms in the Creed imply the disclaimers subjoined, and that all who confess the terms, will certainly understand the disclaimers. But those who both dissent from the latter and impugn the former, such men are proved on every side to be foes of Christ."
Coptic Pope & Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

In the very next paragraph the same Athanasius, who suffered five exiles for Nicaea, separates the persons who reject the faith from persons who hold the faith but stumble over a word:

St Athanasius
De Synodis 41 (AD 359)
"Those who deny the Council altogether, are sufficiently exposed by these brief remarks; those, however, who accept everything else that was defined at Nicæa, and doubt only about the Coessential, must not be treated as enemies; nor do we here attack them as Ario-maniacs, nor as opponents of the Fathers, but we discuss the matter with them as brothers with brothers, who mean what we mean, and dispute only about the word."
Coptic Pope & Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

This is the first key to the whole question. The anathema on Arianism did not move an inch. But Athanasius did not apply it to people who held the substance of the Nicene faith while hesitating over its wording. He judged persons by what they actually believed.

Who Counts as a Heretic

St Augustine of Hippo
Letter 43.1 (c. AD 397)
"But though the doctrine which men hold be false and perverse, if they do not maintain it with passionate obstinacy, especially when they have not devised it by the rashness of their own presumption, but have accepted it from parents who had been misguided and had fallen into error, and if they are with anxiety seeking the truth, and are prepared to be set right when they have found it, such men are not to be counted heretics."
Western FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Augustine goes on to say that even with an obstinate heretic, "we do not refuse to strive by every means in our power for his correction." The founder who invents an error and the child raised in it are not in the same position, even though the error is the same.

A Church Sentence, Not a Private Curse

The canons treat anathema as a sentence of the Church concerning communion. The Council of Ephesus (431), in its seventh canon, ruled that anyone who composed a rival creed or taught the doctrines of Nestorius should be deposed if a bishop or cleric, "and if they be laymen, they shall be anathematized." Deposition is what the Church does to clergy; anathema is what she does to laymen. Both are acts of the Church about a person's place in her life.

The Harsh Side, Honestly

None of this makes heresy safe in the Fathers' eyes. St Basil, writing about the Montanists, who gave the title of the Paraclete to their founder and his prophetess, did not soften his words:

St Basil the Great
Letter 188, canon 1 (c. AD 374)
"They are thus also liable to eternal damnation, inasmuch as blasphemy against the Holy Ghost admits of no forgiveness."
Cappadocian FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

The Fathers believed separation from the true faith was deadly. That is exactly why they used the anathema, and exactly why they worked so hard to bring people back across it. A page that hid this would not be honest, and it would also make the rest of the evidence less impressive. The same men who wrote these sentences are the ones who designed the gentle ways of receiving converts in the next two sections.

The Second Century: Irenaeus Rebukes Victor

Around 190, Victor, bishop of Rome, tried to cut off the churches of Asia because they kept Pascha on a different day. Eusebius records that Victor "declared all the brethren there wholly excommunicate," and that "this did not please all the bishops." Among those who rebuked him was St Irenaeus of Lyons:

St Irenaeus, quoted by Eusebius
Church History V.24.13
"Yet all of these lived none the less in peace, and we also live in peace with one another; and the disagreement in regard to the fast confirms the agreement in the faith."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

From the beginning the Church distinguished between unity of faith, which cannot bend, and unity of practice, which can.

The Third Century: Cyprian, Stephen and Dionysius

The first great argument about receiving converts came in the 250s. St Cyprian of Carthage and the African councils held that anyone coming from heresy or schism must be baptized, because outside the Church there is no true baptism. Stephen of Rome held the opposite rule. Cyprian quotes Stephen's letter:

Stephen of Rome, quoted by St Cyprian
Cyprian, Epistle 73 (to Pompey)
"If any one, therefore, come to you from any heresy whatever, let nothing be innovated (or done) which has not been handed down, to wit, that hands be imposed on him for repentance"
Bishop of RomeVERBATIM · ANF TRANSLATION

Cyprian answered with a line that still has force: "custom without truth is the antiquity of error" (Epistle 73). He was not a man who took truth lightly. Yet look at how he opened the council of 256:

St Cyprian of Carthage
Seventh Council of Carthage (AD 256)
"It remains, that upon this same matter each of us should bring forward what we think, judging no man, nor rejecting any one from the right of communion, if he should think differently from us. For neither does any of us set himself up as a bishop of bishops..."
Bishop & MartyrVERBATIM · ANF TRANSLATION
St Cyprian and his colleagues
Epistle 71, to Stephen
"In which behalf we neither do violence to, nor impose a law upon, any one, since each prelate has in the administration of the Church the exercise of his will free, as he shall give an account of his conduct to the Lord."
Bishop & MartyrVERBATIM · ANF TRANSLATION

Stephen went further. Dionysius of Alexandria reports that he had written about the bishops of Cilicia, Cappadocia and Galatia, "saying that he would not commune with them for this same cause; namely, that they re-baptized heretics" (Eusebius, Church History VII.5.4).

The Pope of Alexandria in those years, St Dionysius, stood between them, and what he did matters for the Coptic Church especially. Alexandria had its own rule, which he had received from his predecessor Heraclas. It concerned people who had been baptized in the Church, drifted into a heretical group and wanted to return:

St Dionysius of Alexandria
Quoted by Eusebius, Church History VII.7.4
"...he did not receive them back, though they entreated for it, until they had publicly reported all things which they had heard from their adversaries; but then he received them without requiring of them another baptism. For they had formerly received the Holy Spirit from him."
Coptic Pope & Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Yet he refused to force his rule on the churches that rebaptized:

St Dionysius of Alexandria
Quoted by Eusebius, Church History VII.7.5
"I have learned also that this is not a novel practice introduced in Africa alone, but that even long ago in the times of the bishops before us this opinion has been adopted in the most populous churches, and in synods of the brethren in Iconium and Synnada, and by many others. To overturn their counsels and throw them into strife and contention, I cannot endure."
Coptic Pope & Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

And when a man in his own flock, who had long ago been baptized by heretics and had received Communion for years, begged to be baptized properly, Dionysius would not do it:

St Dionysius of Alexandria
Letter to Xystus of Rome, in Eusebius, Church History VII.9.4
"But I did not dare to do this; and said that his long communion was sufficient for this. For I should not dare to renew from the beginning one who had heard the giving of thanks and joined in repeating the Amen; who had stood by the table and had stretched forth his hands to receive the blessed food; and who had received it, and partaken for a long while of the body and blood of our Lord Jesus Christ."
Coptic Pope & Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

So in the third century there were holy bishops with different practices. Cyprian rebaptized but would not break communion over it. Stephen did not rebaptize and threatened to break communion with those who did. Dionysius kept his own gentler rule and refused to fight the stricter churches. None of them changed the faith. The later Church did not simply pick a winner. It learned to sort cases by what each group believed and how it baptized.

The Council of Nicaea: Two Rules in One Council

First Council of Nicaea
Canon 8 (AD 325)
"Concerning those who call themselves Cathari, if they come over to the Catholic and Apostolic Church, the great and holy Synod decrees that they who are ordained shall continue as they are in the clergy. But it is before all things necessary that they should profess in writing that they will observe and follow the dogmas of the Catholic and Apostolic Church..."
Ecumenical CouncilVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION
First Council of Nicaea
Canon 19 (AD 325)
"Concerning the Paulianists who have flown for refuge to the Catholic Church, it has been decreed that they must by all means be rebaptized..."
Ecumenical CouncilVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

The Cathari (Novatians) were strict rigorists, but they held the Nicene faith about God. Their clergy were kept in their rank after a written profession. The Paulianists followed Paul of Samosata, who, as St Severus later put it, "sinned against the very substance of God the Word." They were baptized. One council, two rules, one principle: what was the group's faith?

St Basil: Strict Theology, Gentle Practice, in One Letter

St Basil's first canonical letter to Amphilochius of Iconium became one of the foundations of the Church's canon law. He opens by agreeing that "it is right to follow the custom obtaining in each region, because those, who at the time gave decision on these points, held different opinions concerning their baptism." Then he explains the old categories:

St Basil the Great
Letter 188, canon 1 (c. AD 374)
"The old authorities decided to accept that baptism which in nowise errs from the faith. Thus they used the names of heresies, of schisms, and of unlawful congregations. By heresies they meant men who were altogether broken off and alienated in matters relating to the actual faith; by schisms men who had separated for some ecclesiastical reasons and questions capable of mutual solution; by unlawful congregations gatherings held by disorderly presbyters or bishops or by uninstructed laymen."
Cappadocian FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION
St Basil the Great
Letter 188, canon 1
"So it seemed good to the ancient authorities to reject the baptism of heretics altogether, but to admit that of schismatics, on the ground that they still belonged to the Church."
Cappadocian FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Next he states the strict theology of Cyprian and of his own predecessor Firmilian: that when the separatists broke away, "those who had apostatized from the Church had no longer on them the grace of the Holy Spirit, for it ceased to be imparted when the continuity was broken." He does not dismiss that reasoning. And then, in the very next sentence:

St Basil the Great
Letter 188, canon 1
"Nevertheless, since it has seemed to some of those of Asia that, for the sake of management of the majority, their baptism should be accepted, let it be accepted."
Cappadocian FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Later in the same canon, discussing the Encratites, whose baptism he would rather reject, he explains why he will still fall back on custom if strictness would do harm:

St Basil the Great
Letter 188, canon 1
"For I am under some apprehension lest, in our wish to discourage them from baptizing, we may, through the severity of our decision, be a hindrance to those who are being saved."
Cappadocian FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

In one letter Basil states the strict theology, accepts the gentler practice, names the Montanists as liable to damnation, and says his aim is not to hinder "those who are being saved." He saw no contradiction in any of it.

St Augustine: Real but Unprofitable Outside

In the West, St Augustine worked out the other explanation during the Donatist controversy. A baptism given in the name of the Trinity outside the Church is a real baptism, so it is not repeated. But it does a person no good while he stays outside by choice. He calls it a sacrament "which can indeed be received but cannot profit elsewhere," and he describes what happens at reconciliation:

St Augustine of Hippo
On Baptism, Against the Donatists, Book I (c. AD 400)
"But as, by reconciliation to unity, that begins to be profitably possessed which was possessed to no profit in exclusion from unity..."
Western FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

On this view, too, recognizing a baptism is not approving the body that gave it.

This section holds the key to the whole question. Look at what the gentle way of receiving converts actually contained.

An order of reception that the later Church counted as the seventh canon of the Council of Constantinople (381) sets out all three ways side by side. Its NPNF editor, following earlier scholars, notes that it was probably not passed in 381 but comes from a fifth-century letter of the church of Constantinople. The Council in Trullo (692) later adopted it almost word for word in its canon 95.

"Canon 7" of Constantinople
Order of reception (5th century)
"Those who from heresy turn to orthodoxy, and to the portion of those who are being saved, we receive according to the following method and custom: Arians, and Macedonians, and Sabbatians, and Novatians, who call themselves Cathari or Aristori, and Quarto-decimans or Tetradites, and Apollinarians, we receive, upon their giving a written renunciation [of their errors] and anathematize every heresy which is not in accordance with the Holy, Catholic, and Apostolic Church of God. Thereupon, they are first sealed or anointed with the holy oil upon the forehead, eyes, nostrils, mouth, and ears; and when we seal them, we say, The Seal of the gift of the Holy Ghost."
CanonVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION
"Canon 7" of Constantinople
Order of reception, continued
"But Eunomians, who are baptized with only one immersion, and Montanists, who are here called Phrygians, and Sabellians, who teach the identity of Father and Son... all these, when they desire to turn to orthodoxy, we receive as heathen... and then we baptize them."
CanonVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Two things stand out. First, the reasons for the strict way are given: a corrupted faith in the Trinity, or a changed form of baptism ("only one immersion"). The line is drawn by faith and form, not by how hostile a group was. Arians were the Church's bitterest enemies in the fourth century, yet they were received by chrism.

Second, and this answers the question at the top of the page: the gentle way begins with the convert himself anathematizing "every heresy which is not in accordance with the Holy, Catholic, and Apostolic Church of God." The anathema is not the opposite of receiving someone without a new baptism. It is how he was received.

Ephesus, 431: Anathema Alone

When the Council of Ephesus dealt with a group who had been taught a Nestorian creed, it did not baptize them or even anoint them. St Severus, who knew the acts of the council, describes the decision:

St Severus of Antioch
Select Letters V.14, to the bishops on the hill of Mardē (from exile, 520s)
"...it both received him and admitted him to clemency, not making any canonical pronouncement in their case about chrism or about anything else of this kind: but it lawfully decided that they were to renounce the heresy itself in writing and condemn it by anathema, and so be received and become members of Christ's flock."
Patriarch of Antioch & Church FatherVERBATIM · BROOKS TRANSLATION (1904)

The Chalcedonian Side Did the Same

The Council in Trullo (692), which the Oriental Orthodox do not receive, repeats the order above and then adds a further class of converts:

Council in Trullo
Canon 95 (AD 692)
"...must give certificates and anathematize each his own heresy, and also Nestorius, Eutyches, Dioscorus, Severus, and the other chiefs of such heresies, and those who think with them, and all the aforesaid heresies; and so they become partakers of the holy Communion."
Chalcedonian CouncilVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

The received text of this sentence is garbled, and its NPNF editor prints Hefele's argument that a line has dropped out. On the corrected reading, the later Christological parties are the ones received by a written certificate and anathema, without a new baptism. Read either way, the passage shows two things at once. The Chalcedonian Church anathematized our fathers by name, and it received those who came over from these divisions by a written anathema, not by baptizing them again. Each side, at its sharpest, still did not treat the other's baptism as nothing.

The question on this page is not new. It was put to the Oriental Orthodox Fathers in almost the same words fifteen centuries ago, and their answer has survived.

After 451 the bishops who rejected Chalcedon had to decide how to receive clergy and people who had been in communion with it. St Timothy Aelurus, Pope of Alexandria (457–477, much of it in exile), at first said he would not receive clergy ordained by Proterius, the Chalcedonian bishop the imperial government had installed in Alexandria. Later he received them on repentance, in their orders. Some of the faithful accused him of contradicting himself. St Severus, before he became Patriarch of Antioch, answered them in a letter to John the tribune, written in the name of Father Leontius (between 489 and 508).

St Severus of Antioch
Select Letters V.1, to John the tribune
"But you say that the holy Timothy contradicted himself, seeing that at first he said that those who had been ordained should not be received, but at a later time received them."
Patriarch of Antioch & Church FatherVERBATIM · BROOKS TRANSLATION (1904)

He replies with examples from the apostles, such as Peter and the Gentiles in Acts, and then copies out a passage from St John Chrysostom's encomium on St Paul:

St John Chrysostom, quoted by St Severus
Select Letters V.1
"And he would sacrifice, and shave: and again he would anathematize the doers of these things. And now he would circumcize: now he would reject circumcision. And the things done were contradictory: but the thought and intention from which these proceeded was very consistent and consonant with itself. For he sought one thing, the salvation of those who saw and heard these things."
Church FatherVERBATIM · BROOKS TRANSLATION (1904)
St John Chrysostom, quoted by St Severus
Select Letters V.1
"For in fact in the case of a physician also, when you see him now burning now feeding; and using now iron now medicine; and now prohibiting foods and drinks, now allowing the patient to enjoy these without stint... you do not blame him for the variation, nor because of the perpetual change: but you will then praise the technical skill the more..."
Church FatherVERBATIM · BROOKS TRANSLATION (1904)

Then Severus turns to the very baptism controversy described in Section V:

St Severus of Antioch
Select Letters V.1
"...when Cyprian in Africa with the synod under him said that all who had been baptized by heresy ought to receive the laver of regeneration, as if they had never originally been baptized at all, but at last the opinion of Sixtus of Rome, and of Dionysius of Alexandria and of the others prevailed, so that those who had been baptized by heretics in the name of the three substances, the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, were not re-baptized, no one of sound intelligence said that those saintly bishops contradicted themselves, but that upon later consideration they chose a better and more canonical course"

As Patriarch: Different Medicines for Different Diseases

As patriarch (512–518), Severus wrote against those who wanted converts from Chalcedon to be re-anointed with chrism. The letter's title states his position: it is "against those who say that men who have communicated with the Synod of Chalcedon, and are penitent and anathematize those who call our one Lord and God Jesus Christ two natures after the ineffable union and come over to the orthodox faith must be re-anointed."

"Those who assembled in Africa in the days of Cyprian, looking at the common name of the heresies only, applied a single method of cure. But the contemporaries of the great Dionysius and those who came after him distinguished the varieties of diseases, and so applied to each of them the cure suited for it."

"As it seemed necessary to the men of earlier days that converts from heresies should be perfected through baptism, but afterwards a sagacious discrimination regarding the matter introduced the principle that some must be re-baptized, others re-anointed, so concerning the Nestorian heresy the fathers after examining the character of the heresy decided that the impious doctrine should only be anathematized by those who repent."

He adds that after Ephesus "nor has anyone been shown to have been anointed at that time," even though the bishops of the East had supported Nestorius for a long while before the reunion of 433. And he notes that Timothy, when he received the clergy of Proterius, "did not give them ordination at all."

From Exile: What the Anathema Does

Later, from exile, Severus wrote to two bishops, Sergius of Cyrrhus and Marion of Sura, about how to receive people baptized by Chalcedonians. This passage is the clearest answer in the Fathers to the question of this page, so it is given in full.

St Severus of Antioch
Select Letters V.15, to Sergius and Marion
"No one is so devoid of the sacred teachings of orthodoxy as not to call baptism given by any heresy imperfect and spurious: but those who know this look at the regulations of the holy fathers, and reflect that according to the character of the heresy treatment also must be applied, and the stain must be purged and expiated either by another baptism or by chrism, or by an anathema of the heresy, and the judgment of the orthodox priests and reception by them."
Patriarch of Antioch & Church FatherVERBATIM · BROOKS TRANSLATION (1904)

"After the same fashion also an anathema of the heresy, when enjoined upon those who utter the condemnation by order of orthodox high-priests, and proffered by the mouth of those, as well as entrance into the holy of holies performed in accordance with the judgment of those who receive them brings healing to the sore, and causes the pure and sincere grace of the Spirit to flash invisibly upon those who have been accepted."

What Severus Teaches

Put together, the Oriental Orthodox Father gives a complete answer:

  • A baptism given in heresy is not complete. He calls it "imperfect and spurious." Economy is not indifference.
  • It is still not repeated when the faith in the Trinity and the form were kept. To re-baptize or even re-anoint such converts was a fault, and a bishop was expelled for it.
  • The anathema is the medicine. The convert's own anathema of the heresy, with the bishop's reception, "brings healing to the sore" and the grace of the Spirit.
  • Changes in practice are not contradictions when they serve the same end. The Fathers' "one purpose was the salvation of those who had perished."

Within the Oriental Orthodox Family

The Coptic, Syriac, Armenian, Ethiopian, Eritrean and Malankara churches are in full communion with one another. A member of one who moves to another is not received at all in this sense. He simply goes to church.

Toward other churches, practice differs from one Oriental Orthodox church to another.

Coptic Orthodox

Under Pope Shenouda III (1971–2012) the Coptic Church baptized converts coming from the Catholic and Protestant churches. In his Comparative Theology he gives his reasons with regard to Protestants: their baptism is not given by an ordained priest, is not held to be a sacrament, and is often not by immersion. SUMMARY In 2001 the Coptic Orthodox and Greek Orthodox Patriarchates of Alexandria agreed to recognize each other's baptism.

Armenian Apostolic

Armenian parish teaching describes the Church as accepting baptism from other churches that confess the Holy Trinity, so such converts are not baptized again. SUMMARY

Syriac Orthodox

In 1984 Patriarch Zakka I and Pope John Paul II agreed that where their faithful cannot reach their own priest, they may "ask for the Sacraments of Penance, Eucharist and Anointing of the Sick from lawful priests of either of our two sister Churches." The same text says that the Eucharist "cannot yet be concelebrated by us."

The difference is discussed inside the family. The British Orthodox Church, while it was part of the Coptic Patriarchate, sent a representation to the Coptic Holy Synod arguing that the older Alexandrian practice, going back to Timothy, Philoxenus and Severus, was to receive Chalcedonians by confession of faith and prayer, that requiring Catholics to be baptized is a recent development, and that the Armenian, Syriac and Indian churches still keep the older practice. SUMMARY This page takes no side between sister churches on a question of discipline. It notes only that the patristic evidence in Section VII is the evidence that representation relied on.

In 2017 Pope Tawadros II and Pope Francis signed a common declaration. Early news reports had them saying they "will not repeat" each other's baptisms. The official text is more careful: they "mutually declare that we, with one mind and heart, will seek sincerely not to repeat the baptism that has been administered in either of our Churches for any person who wishes to join the other." The Coptic Information Center then stated that the declaration "does not contain a formal agreement on the question of re-baptisms."

The Eastern Orthodox

The Eastern Orthodox have moved back and forth in the same way. A council at Constantinople in 1484 received Latins by renunciation and chrismation, and the great Council of Moscow in 1667 forbade rebaptizing them. In 1755 Patriarch Cyril V of Constantinople, with the patriarchs of Alexandria and Jerusalem, required them to be baptized, a decision the Russian Church never adopted. St Nicodemus of the Holy Mountain, in his commentary on the canons (the Pedalion, 1800), defended the strict view and explained the older, gentler practice as economy. Today most Eastern Orthodox churches receive Catholics and many Protestants by chrismation or confession, while some communities, notably on Mount Athos, baptize. SUMMARY

Between Rome and Constantinople, the sentences of 1054 were addressed in 1965. Pope Paul VI and Patriarch Athenagoras said of the authors of those sentences, "They had directed their censures against the persons concerned and not the Churches," and declared that they "regret and remove both from memory and from the midst of the Church the sentences of excommunication." The same declaration says the gesture "is not sufficient to end both old and more recent differences." Communion was not restored.

Between the Two Orthodox Families

The official dialogue between the Eastern and Oriental Orthodox concluded in its Second Agreed Statement (Chambésy, 1990):

Joint Commission, Eastern and Oriental Orthodox
Second Agreed Statement, Chambésy (1990)
"Both families agree that all the anathemas and condemnations of the past which now divide us should be lifted by the Churches in order that the last obstacle to the full unity and communion of our two families can be removed by the grace and power of God."
Official DialogueVERBATIM · OFFICIAL TEXT

The next point of the statement explains on what basis: "the lifting of anathemas and condemnations will be consummated on the basis that the Councils and Fathers previously anathematized or condemned are not heretical." That is exactly Athanasius's principle in De Synodis 41: people who mean what we mean are not to be treated as enemies. Nobody proposed lifting the anathemas on Nestorianism or Eutychianism. A 1993 follow-up proposed that the lifting should be done by all the churches together, at the same moment, with full communion restored at once. That has not yet happened, and there has been strong opposition, especially from the monks of Mount Athos (see Eastern & Oriental Orthodoxy).

The objection can be put as an argument:

  1. An anathema cuts a person off from God and from salvation.
  2. Accepting a person's baptism treats him as already joined to God.
  3. The Church does both to the same people.
  4. So the Church contradicts herself.

Premise 1 is half true. The anathema is severe, and the Fathers meant it. It declares that a teaching is false and that whoever holds it in obstinacy stands outside the Church's communion, which the Fathers believed to be a deadly place. But it is aimed at a teaching and at those who knowingly persist in it (Athanasius, Augustine). It is a sentence of the Church about communion, it can be lifted, and Scripture leaves the final judgment of those outside to God (1 Corinthians 5:13).

Premise 2 is not what the Fathers meant. Not repeating a baptism does not say the person is in communion, or that his baptism has done all it should. Augustine says it was possessed "to no profit" outside unity. Severus calls it "imperfect and spurious." Even on the most generous Orthodox view, recognizing a baptism is not recognizing the body that gave it as the Church in its fullness.

Premise 3 gets the order wrong. The Church does not anathematize and accept the same person at the same moment. The anathema stands against the error. The person who comes is received by joining in that anathema himself. In the ancient rite the two things the objection sets against each other are two halves of one act, and Severus says the anathema is the very means by which the grace of the Spirit is given.

What Stays Fixed and What Varies

Fixed

The faith itself. The anathema on heresy as such. Baptism is one and is not repeated when truly given in the name of the Trinity with the right form. No one enters communion without confessing the Orthodox faith. The aim of all discipline is the salvation of souls.

Varies

Which groups' baptism is accepted. Which of the three ways of reception is used. The tone of relations and how much pastoral cooperation is allowed. How quickly anathemas against persons are lifted once agreement in faith is found.

Everything in the second column has varied since the third century, with saints on more than one side. Basil called it "the custom obtaining in each region." Severus called it different medicines for different diseases. Everything in the first column has not moved.

Where Real Tension Remains

An honest answer has to name the places where the picture is not tidy.

1. The same convert may be received differently by different Orthodox churches. A Catholic may be chrismated or received by confession in one church and baptized in another. The Fathers expected regional differences, and both churches confess the same faith and share one Eucharist. But it is fair to admit that this sends mixed signals, that the churches have not harmonized their practice, and that the question is still discussed inside the Coptic Church itself.

2. There are two Orthodox explanations of economy, and no council has chosen between them. On the strict view (Cyprian, Firmilian, St Nicodemus, and in the twentieth century Metropolitan Anthony Khrapovitsky), outside the Church the rite is an empty form, and when the Church receives a convert without baptism she fills that form with grace at the moment of reception. On the other view (Augustine, and among modern Orthodox Fr Georges Florovsky), the rite is real but unfulfilled outside unity. Florovsky wrote: "In her sacramental, mysterious being the Church surpasses all canonical norms." And again: "All that Cyprian said about the unity of the Church and the sacraments can be and must be accepted. But it is not necessary to draw with him the final boundary around the body of the Church by means of canonical points alone." Severus's language, "imperfect and spurious" yet healed through anathema and reception, sits between the two. Either explanation removes the contradiction. Neither says that non-Orthodox Christians are under a personal curse, and neither says that separation does not matter.

3. Some modern gestures run ahead of the churches. Agreed statements of a dialogue commission are recommendations, not synodal decisions. The 1990 call to lift anathemas has not been carried out, and the 2017 wording was softened from "will not" to "will seek sincerely not to." Where a modern statement seems to say more than the Church has decided, the honest reading is that it has not yet been received. The Church's slowness here is not inconsistency; it is the same caution the Fathers showed.

4. Not every past sentence on persons was just. Anger and politics were mixed into the schisms. The 1965 declaration speaks of "the offensive words, the reproaches without foundation, and the reprehensible gestures which, on both sides, have marked or accompanied the sad events of this period." The Church does not claim that every anathema of a named person was rightly aimed. That is why such anathemas can be lifted when agreement in faith is found, while the anathemas on heresy stay.

The Verdict

In doctrine, the Church is not inconsistent. The harshness and the generosity come from the same conviction: that the true faith matters for salvation. A Church that did not care about truth would never anathematize. A Church that did not care about souls would never use economy. The Fathers who wrote the anathemas are the same Fathers who designed the gentle ways of reception, and they put the anathema inside the rite of reception itself.

In practice, there is variety. Some of it is ancient and deliberate. Some of it is unresolved. The honest thing is to say which is which, and the Fathers give us the tools to do that. St Severus faced this exact charge and answered it: "Though these things seem contradictory to many, yet nevertheless their one purpose was the salvation of those who had perished."

Romans 9: Election & the Potter

"As then Pharaoh became a vessel of wrath by his own lawlessness, so did these become vessels of mercy by their own readiness to obey." St John Chrysostom, Homily 16 on Romans

Ura Kidane MehretEthiopian Orthodox · Lake Tana

Few chapters of the Bible have been read with more fear than Romans 9. "Jacob I have loved, but Esau I have hated." "He has mercy on whom He wills, and whom He wills He hardens." "Does not the potter have power over the clay?" From these verses St Augustine in his later years, and after him John Calvin and the Synod of Dort, drew the doctrine that God, before the world began, chose some people for eternal life and passed over the rest, without regard to anything He foresaw in them. Many sincere Christians have read Romans 9 and concluded that their eternal destiny was fixed before they were born.

The Orthodox Church reads the chapter differently, and so did the Church before Augustine. That reading does not make God smaller. It takes seriously that Romans 9 is the first part of a single argument running to the end of chapter 11, that it is about God's dealings with Israel and the nations, that Paul himself says why Israel stumbled, and that the image of the potter has a history in Scripture. The most important part of that history is the prophet Jeremiah's visit to the potter's house, where God explained the image Himself.

This page sets out both readings in full, gives the Reformed position in the words of Calvin, Westminster and Dort, shows that Protestants themselves have never agreed on it, and then makes the case from Jeremiah 18, St Paul's own words in 2 Timothy, and the Fathers. It also says honestly where the case is strong and where it needs care.

"As then Pharaoh became a vessel of wrath by his own lawlessness, so did these become vessels of mercy by their own readiness to obey." St John Chrysostom, Homily 16 on Romans

Election
ἐκλογή · eklogē
Choosing. In Romans 9:11, God's choice of Jacob's line over Esau's.
Purpose
πρόθεσις · prothesis
A plan set forth beforehand (9:11; 8:28).
Hated
ἐμίσησα · emisēsa
"I hated" (9:13, quoting Malachi 1:3). In Hebrew idiom, to love one and "hate" another often means to choose or prefer one over the other (Genesis 29:30–31; Luke 14:26).
Harden
σκληρύνω · sklērynō
To make hard or stubborn (9:18). Used of Pharaoh, who is also said to have hardened his own heart.
Potter
κεραμεύς · kerameus
The craftsman who shapes clay (9:21). The image comes from Isaiah, Jeremiah and Wisdom.
Lump
φύραμα · phyrama
A batch of kneaded clay or dough (9:21; 11:16).
Vessel
σκεῦος · skeuos
A pot or container, and by extension a person as God's instrument (9:21–23; 2 Timothy 2:20–21; Acts 9:15).
Prepared, fitted
κατηρτισμένα · katērtismena
"Vessels of wrath prepared for destruction" (9:22). A perfect participle with no stated agent: Paul does not say who fitted them.
Prepared beforehand
προητοίμασεν · proētoimasen
"Vessels of mercy, which He had prepared beforehand for glory" (9:23). Here God is the stated subject. The difference from 9:22 is noticed by many commentators.
Longsuffering

Patience that holds back anger. God "endured with much longsuffering the vessels of wrath" (9:22), the same patience that leads to repentance (2:4).
Romans 9:1-3
"I tell the truth in Christ, I am not lying, my conscience also bearing me witness in the Holy Spirit, that I have great sorrow and continual grief in my heart. For I could wish that I myself were accursed from Christ for my brethren, my countrymen according to the flesh,"
🔵 Orthodox reading: Paul begins not with a theory of predestination but with grief. He would be cut off himself if it would save his people.
Romans 9:4-5
"who are Israelites, to whom pertain the adoption, the glory, the covenants, the giving of the law, the service of God, and the promises; of whom are the fathers and from whom, according to the flesh, Christ came, who is over all, the eternally blessed God. Amen."
🔵 Orthodox reading: Israel's privileges were real, and from them came Christ, "who is over all, the eternally blessed God."
Romans 9:6-8
"But it is not that the word of God has taken no effect. For they are not all Israel who are of Israel, nor are they all children because they are the seed of Abraham; but, 'In Isaac your seed shall be called.' That is, those who are the children of the flesh, these are not the children of God; but the children of the promise are counted as the seed."
🔵 Orthodox reading: The question of Romans 9 is whether God's word to Israel has failed. Paul answers that the true Israel has always been the children of the promise.

Everything in Romans 9 must be read in the light of this question. Paul has just said that nothing can separate God's people from His love (8:39). But most of Israel, God's own people, have not believed in their Messiah. Has God's promise failed? Romans 9 to 11 is one answer in three parts, and it ends not with a fixed number of the elect and the reprobate but with "God has committed them all to disobedience, that He might have mercy on all" (11:32).

Chrysostom saw the shape of the argument clearly. Paul is answering a Jewish objection, and he answers it by showing that God has always chosen freely within Abraham's family, and is free now to show mercy to Gentiles who believe:

St John Chrysostom
Homily 16 on Romans (on 9:6–8)
"Now if all have sinned, how come some to be saved, and some to perish? It is because all were not minded to come to Him, since for His part all were saved, for all were called."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION
St John Chrysostom
Homily 16 on Romans (on 9:8–9)
"Thus are we also gendered by the words of God. Since in the pool of water it is the words of God which generate and fashion us. For it is by being baptized into the Name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Ghost that we are gendered. And this birth is not of nature, but of the promise of God."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION
Romans 9:10-13
"And not only this, but when Rebecca also had conceived by one man, even by our father Isaac (for the children not yet being born, nor having done any good or evil, that the purpose of God according to election might stand, not of works but of Him who calls), it was said to her, 'The older shall serve the younger.' As it is written, 'Jacob I have loved, but Esau I have hated.'"
🔵 Orthodox reading: Before the twins were born or had done anything, God chose the younger to carry the promise. Election is God's free gift, not a wage for works.
Genesis 25:23
"And the Lord said to her: 'Two nations are in your womb, Two peoples shall be separated from your body; One people shall be stronger than the other, And the older shall serve the younger.'"
🔵 Orthodox reading: What God told Rebecca was about two nations: the people descended from Esau would serve the people descended from Jacob.
Malachi 1:2-3
"'I have loved you,' says the Lord. 'Yet you say, 'In what way have You loved us?' Was not Esau Jacob's brother?' Says the Lord. 'Yet Jacob I have loved; But Esau I have hated, And laid waste his mountains and his heritage For the jackals of the wilderness.'"
🔵 Orthodox reading: Malachi, writing more than a thousand years after the twins, speaks of the nation of Edom and its land.

Three things about these verses are often missed.

First, they are about peoples and roles in history. Genesis 25:23 speaks of "two nations" and "two peoples," and the promise was that "the older shall serve the younger," a matter of which line would carry the promise to Christ. Malachi speaks of Edom's mountains laid waste. Neither passage says anything about Esau's soul or his eternal destiny. Even Calvin, who reads the verses as pointing to eternal election, admits that in Malachi "earthly blessings are there recorded" and that the Lord "rejected the Edomites, the progeny of Esau."

John Calvin
Commentary on Romans (on 9:13)
"I therefore chose you for my people, that I might show the same kindness to the seed of Jacob; but I rejected the Edomites, the progeny of Esau... Now, though earthly blessings are there recorded, which God had conferred on the Israelites, it is not yet right to view them but as symbols of his benevolence..."
ReformerVERBATIM · OWEN TRANSLATION (1849)

Second, "hated" is Hebrew idiom for choosing one over another. When Jacob "loved Rachel more than Leah," Scripture immediately says Leah was "unloved," literally "hated" (Genesis 29:30–31). Christ says no one can be His disciple who does not "hate" his father and mother (Luke 14:26), which Matthew renders "loves father or mother more than Me" (Matthew 10:37).

Genesis 29:30-31
"Then Jacob also went in to Rachel, and he also loved Rachel more than Leah. And he served with Laban still another seven years. When the Lord saw that Leah was unloved, He opened her womb; but Rachel was barren."
🔵 Orthodox reading: Leah was not the object of Jacob's malice; she was simply not chosen as Rachel was.
Deuteronomy 23:7
"You shall not abhor an Edomite, for he is your brother. You shall not abhor an Egyptian, because you were an alien in his land."
🔵 Orthodox reading: God's law forbade Israel to abhor the Edomites: "he is your brother." Esau's descendants were not the objects of divine loathing.
Genesis 33:9
"But Esau said, 'I have enough, my brother; keep what you have for yourself.'"
🔵 Orthodox reading: Esau himself was blessed with plenty and forgave his brother. His story in Genesis is not the story of a man cursed by God.

Third, Paul's point is that the choice was not earned. "Not of works but of Him who calls." The Orthodox agree completely: no one is chosen because of merit. Chrysostom adds that God, unlike us, does not need to wait for the outcome to know a person's character:

St John Chrysostom
Homily 16 on Romans (on 9:11–13)
"With what intent then did God say this? Because He does not wait, as man does, to see from the issue of their acts the good and him who is not so, but even before these He knows which is the wicked and which not such."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Reformed readers object that this makes election depend on foreseen character, which Paul's "not of works" excludes. That is a fair objection to a crude version of the view. But foreknowing how a person will freely respond to grace is not the same as rewarding his works. Faith is not a work that earns (4:4–5), and the Fathers never said God chose anyone because they deserved it. They said God's choices are not arbitrary, because He knows the heart.

Romans 9:14-16
"What shall we say then? Is there unrighteousness with God? Certainly not! For He says to Moses, 'I will have mercy on whomever I will have mercy, and I will have compassion on whomever I will have compassion.' So then it is not of him who wills, nor of him who runs, but of God who shows mercy."
🔵 Orthodox reading: God's mercy is His to give. No one can claim it by willing or running.
Exodus 33:19
"Then He said, 'I will make all My goodness pass before you, and I will proclaim the name of the Lord before you. I will be gracious to whom I will be gracious, and I will have compassion on whom I will have compassion.'"
🔵 Orthodox reading: The words were spoken to Moses after the golden calf, when all Israel had sinned and God chose to show mercy. They are a revelation of God's goodness, not of arbitrary choice.
St John Chrysostom
Homily 16 on Romans (on 9:15)
"For it is not yours to know, O Moses, he means, which are deserving of My love toward man, but leave this to Me. But if Moses had no right to know, much less have we."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

"It is not of him who wills, nor of him who runs" (9:16). St Gregory the Theologian explains how the Fathers read this verse: not as denying our will, but as denying that our will is enough on its own:

St Gregory of Nazianzus
Oration 37.13
"For it is necessary both that we should be our own masters and also that our salvation should be of God. This is why He says not of him that wills; that is, not of him that wills only, nor of him that runs only, but also of God. That shows mercy. Next; since to will also is from God, he has attributed the whole to God with reason."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION
St John Chrysostom
Homily 16 on Romans (on 9:16)
"Because when he says, it is not of him that wills, nor of him that runs, he does not deprive us of free-will, but shows that all is not one's own, for that it requires grace from above. For it is binding on us to will, and also to run: but to confide not in our own labors, but in the love of God toward man."

Who Hardened Pharaoh?

Romans 9:17-18
"For the Scripture says to the Pharaoh, 'For this very purpose I have raised you up, that I may show My power in you, and that My name may be declared in all the earth.' Therefore He has mercy on whom He wills, and whom He wills He hardens."
🔵 Orthodox reading: God raised Pharaoh up to show His power, and He hardens whom He wills.
Exodus 4:21
"And the Lord said to Moses, 'When you go back to Egypt, see that you do all those wonders before Pharaoh which I have put in your hand. But I will harden his heart, so that he will not let the people go."
🔵 Orthodox reading: God foretold that He would harden Pharaoh's heart.
Exodus 8:15
"But when Pharaoh saw that there was relief, he hardened his heart and did not heed them, as the Lord had said."
🔵 Orthodox reading: Yet Exodus also says, again and again, that Pharaoh hardened his own heart.
Exodus 9:12
"But the Lord hardened the heart of Pharaoh; and he did not heed them, just as the Lord had spoken to Moses."
🔵 Orthodox reading: Only after Pharaoh had hardened himself through the earlier plagues does the text say that the Lord hardened him.

The Fathers explained the two statements with a famous image. The same sun that melts wax hardens mud. The sun does not change; the material does. So God's same goodness, shown to Pharaoh in sign after sign, softened some Egyptians who left with Israel and hardened the man who refused it.

Origen
De Principiis III.1.11
"Now it is not incorrect to say that the sun, by one and the same power of its heat, melts wax indeed, but dries up and hardens mud: not that its power operates one way upon mud, and in another way upon wax; but that the qualities of mud and wax are different, although according to nature they are one thing, both being from the earth. In this way, then, one and the same working upon the part of God, which was administered by Moses in signs and wonders, made manifest the hardness of Pharaoh, which he had conceived in the intensity of his wickedness..."
Early Writer (not a saint)VERBATIM · ANF TRANSLATION
St Irenaeus of Lyons
Against Heresies IV.29.1
"For one and the same God [that blesses others] inflicts blindness upon those who do not believe, but who set Him at naught; just as the sun, which is a creature of His, [acts with regard] to those who, by reason of any weakness of the eyes cannot behold his light..."
Early WitnessVERBATIM · ANF TRANSLATION

Origen was a great biblical scholar, but some of his speculations, especially about the pre-existence of souls, were rejected by the Church; Pope Theophilus of Alexandria condemned them around AD 400. He is quoted here only where his reading agrees with the Fathers the Church received.

Romans 9:19-21
"You will say to me then, 'Why does He still find fault? For who has resisted His will?' But indeed, O man, who are you to reply against God? Will the thing formed say to him who formed it, 'Why have you made me like this?' Does not the potter have power over the clay, from the same lump to make one vessel for honor and another for dishonor?"
🔵 Orthodox reading: The objector asks why God finds fault if no one can resist Him. Paul answers that the creature has no standing to put its Maker in the dock.
Romans 9:22-24
"What if God, wanting to show His wrath and to make His power known, endured with much longsuffering the vessels of wrath prepared for destruction, and that He might make known the riches of His glory on the vessels of mercy, which He had prepared beforehand for glory, even us whom He called, not of the Jews only, but also of the Gentiles?"
🔵 Orthodox reading: God endured the vessels of wrath with great patience, and made known His glory to vessels of mercy, called from Jews and Gentiles alike.

Paul's words echo several Old Testament passages. Isaiah twice rebukes the clay for questioning the potter (Isaiah 29:16; 45:9). The Wisdom of Solomon, which the Orthodox Churches read as Scripture, describes a potter making clean and common vessels "of the same clay," the very phrase Paul uses:

Isaiah 45:9
"Woe to him who strives with his Maker! Let the potsherd strive with the potsherds of the earth! Shall the clay say to him who forms it, 'What are you making?' Or shall your handiwork say, 'He has no hands'?"
🔵 Orthodox reading: Isaiah's rebuke was aimed at Israelites who questioned how God chose to save them, through the Persian king Cyrus.
"The potter also tempering soft earth, with labour fashioneth every vessel for our service, and of the same clay he maketh both vessels that are for clean uses, and likewise such as serve to the contrary: but what is the use of these vessels, the potter is the judge." Wisdom 15:7 (Douay-Rheims)

Vessels of Wrath and Vessels of Mercy

Look closely at 9:22–23. Of the vessels of mercy Paul says God "prepared [them] beforehand for glory." Of the vessels of wrath he says only that they were "prepared for destruction," with no agent named, and he says God "endured [them] with much longsuffering." Why would God patiently endure vessels He had Himself made for destruction? Chrysostom answers:

St John Chrysostom
Homily 16 on Romans (on 9:22)
"Pharaoh was a vessel of wrath, that is, a man who by his own hard-heartedness had kindled the wrath of God. For after enjoying much long-suffering, he became no better, but remained unimproved. Wherefore he calls him not only a vessel of wrath, but also one fitted for destruction. That is, fully fitted indeed, but by his own proper self."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION
St John Chrysostom
Homily 16 on Romans (on 9:22)
"Yet still, though God knew this, He endured him with much long-suffering, being willing to bring him to repentance. For had He not willed this, then He would not have been thus long-suffering."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION
St John Chrysostom
Homily 16 on Romans (on 9:23–24)
"As then Pharaoh became a vessel of wrath by his own lawlessness, so did these become vessels of mercy by their own readiness to obey. For though the more part is of God, still they also have contributed themselves some little. Whence he does not say either, vessels of well-doing, or vessels of boldness, but vessels of mercy, to show that the whole is of God."

When Paul asks, "Does not the potter have power over the clay?" he is using an image that God had already explained at length through the prophet Jeremiah. The nineteenth-century English editors of Chrysostom's homilies printed a note at this very verse: "Read Jer. xviii. 1–10." It is good advice. Here is what Jeremiah saw.

Jeremiah 18:1-4
"The word which came to Jeremiah from the Lord, saying: 'Arise and go down to the potter's house, and there I will cause you to hear My words.' Then I went down to the potter's house, and there he was, making something at the wheel. And the vessel that he made of clay was marred in the hand of the potter; so he made it again into another vessel, as it seemed good to the potter to make."
🔵 Orthodox reading: The vessel was spoiled in the potter's hand, and the potter remade it into another vessel. The potter is fully in charge, and what he does responds to the condition of the clay.
Jeremiah 18:5-6
"Then the word of the Lord came to me, saying: 'O house of Israel, can I not do with you as this potter?' says the Lord. 'Look, as the clay is in the potter's hand, so are you in My hand, O house of Israel!"
🔵 Orthodox reading: God applies the image to His people: as the clay is in the potter's hand, so is Israel in His.
Jeremiah 18:7-8
"The instant I speak concerning a nation and concerning a kingdom, to pluck up, to pull down, and to destroy it, if that nation against whom I have spoken turns from its evil, I will relent of the disaster that I thought to bring upon it."
🔵 Orthodox reading: If a nation God has threatened turns from its evil, God relents of the disaster.
Jeremiah 18:9-10
"And the instant I speak concerning a nation and concerning a kingdom, to build and to plant it, if it does evil in My sight so that it does not obey My voice, then I will relent concerning the good with which I said I would benefit it."
🔵 Orthodox reading: And if a nation God has promised to build does evil, God relents of the good He said He would do for it.
Jeremiah 18:11-12
"'Now therefore, speak to the men of Judah and to the inhabitants of Jerusalem, saying, 'Thus says the Lord: 'Behold, I am fashioning a disaster and devising a plan against you. Return now every one from his evil way, and make your ways and your doings good.''' And they said, 'That is hopeless! So we will walk according to our own plans, and we will every one obey the dictates of his evil heart.'"
🔵 Orthodox reading: The potter appeals to the clay: "Return now." The people answer, "That is hopeless! So we will walk according to our own plans."

What the Potter's House Teaches

1. God really is the potter. Nothing in Jeremiah weakens God's sovereignty. "As the clay is in the potter's hand, so are you in My hand." He does with the vessel "as it seemed good to the potter to make." The Orthodox do not deny one word of Romans 9:20–21.

2. The spoiling comes from the clay, not the potter. The vessel was "marred in the hand of the potter." No potter sets out to ruin his own work. When God's people are spoiled, it is because they have refused His hands: "we will walk according to our own plans" (18:12).

3. Our turning really changes how God shapes us. This is the heart of the passage. God says plainly that if a nation turns from evil, He relents of the disaster, and if a nation turns to evil, He relents even of the good He intended (18:8, 10). The potter does not shape us without regard to what we do. Our sin and our repentance genuinely change what He makes of us.

4. And what He makes is always good. The potter is never the problem. Whether He builds or pulls down, remakes or reshapes, He acts in goodness, and even His threats are calls to return: "Return now every one from his evil way" (18:11). A vessel remade after being spoiled is not a lesser work of God. It is the same goodness responding to a different clay. This is exactly what Paul says in Romans 9:22: God "endured with much longsuffering" even the vessels of wrath, and in 2:4, that His longsuffering is meant to lead to repentance.

Read with Jeremiah, Paul's question "Does not the potter have power over the clay?" does not mean "God fixes each person's destiny regardless of anything in them." It means "God has every right to remake Israel, to set aside those who refuse Him and to form a new people from Jews and Gentiles who believe." That is exactly the subject of Romans 9 to 11.

A Note on the Fathers

In fairness, none of the Fathers in the public domain translations consulted for this page quotes Jeremiah 18 when commenting on Romans 9. The connection rests on Scripture itself: Jeremiah 18 is the Old Testament's own explanation of the potter image. But the conclusion the Fathers drew from Romans 9 is the same as Jeremiah's, and two of them reached it by linking Romans 9 to another of Paul's own vessel passages, as the next section shows.

Paul uses the image of honourable and dishonourable vessels in one other place, and there he says what makes the difference:

2 Timothy 2:20-21
"But in a great house there are not only vessels of gold and silver, but also of wood and clay, some for honor and some for dishonor. Therefore if anyone cleanses himself from the latter, he will be a vessel for honor, sanctified and useful for the Master, prepared for every good work."
🔵 Orthodox reading: In a great house there are vessels for honour and dishonour, and "if anyone cleanses himself" he will be a vessel for honour. The same apostle, the same image, and the outcome depends on the vessel's response.

St John of Damascus, who summed up the teaching of the Greek Fathers in the eighth century, puts the two passages side by side:

St John of Damascus
Exposition of the Orthodox Faith IV.19
"It is to be observed that it is the custom in the Holy Scripture to speak of God's permission as His energy, as when the apostle says in the Epistle to the Romans, Hath not the potter power over the clay, of the same lump to make one vessel unto honour and another unto dishonour? And for this reason, that He Himself makes this or that. For He is Himself alone the Maker of all things; yet it is not He Himself that fashions noble or ignoble things, but the personal choice of each one."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION
St John of Damascus
Exposition of the Orthodox Faith IV.19
"And it is evident that the purification must be voluntary: for if a man, he says, purge himself. And the consequent antistrophe responds, If a man purge not himself he will be a vessel to dishonour, unmeet for the master's use and fit only to be broken in pieces."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Origen

"He, accordingly, who purges himself, is made a vessel unto honour, while he who has disdained to cleanse himself from his impurity is made a vessel unto dishonour... For God the Creator makes a certain vessel unto honour, and other vessels to dishonour; but that vessel which has cleansed itself from all impurity He makes a vessel unto honour, while that which has stained itself with the filth of vice He makes a vessel unto dishonour."
Early Writer (not a saint)VERBATIM · ANF TRANSLATION
Origen
De Principiis III.1.22
"...we are not to suppose either that those things which are in our own power can be done without the help of God, or that those which are in God's hand can be brought to completion without the intervention of our acts, and desires, and intention..."

Inside Romans Itself

The same logic runs through Romans 9 to 11. Vessels change:

Romans 9:25-26
"As He says also in Hosea: 'I will call them My people, who were not My people, And her beloved, who was not beloved.' 'And it shall come to pass in the place where it was said to them, 'You are not My people,' There they shall be called sons of the living God.'"
🔵 Orthodox reading: Those who were "not My people" become "My people." Paul applies Hosea to Gentiles who believe. Vessels of dishonour are remade.

🔵 Orthodox reading: Branches broken off for unbelief can be grafted in again "if they do not continue in unbelief," and branches that stand can be cut off if they do not continue in God's goodness.

That is Jeremiah 18 in Paul's own words. The potter's freedom is complete, and He uses it to respond to the clay: cutting off for unbelief, grafting in through faith, and always longing that the spoiled vessel return. This is why the Orthodox say that Romans 9, read to the end of Romans 11, teaches the opposite of a fixed decree of reprobation.

How a Reformed Reader Would Answer Jeremiah 18

A careful Calvinist would reply to the potter's house in four ways. First, Jeremiah 18 is about nations and earthly judgements, not the eternal salvation of individuals. Second, the potter's freedom is the point of both passages: "as it seemed good to the potter." Third, repentance is itself God's gift: "Restore me, and I will return" (Jeremiah 31:18); "God has also granted to the Gentiles repentance to life" (Acts 11:18). So even when the clay turns, it is because the potter turned it, and Dort says exactly this. Fourth, Paul in Romans 9:20 is silencing the objector, not explaining God's reasons.

Jeremiah 31:18
"I have surely heard Ephraim bemoaning himself: 'You have chastised me, and I was chastised, Like an untrained bull; Restore me, and I will return, For You are the Lord my God."
🔵 Orthodox reading: The penitent asks God to restore him so that he can return. Repentance is a gift.

The Orthodox Response

To the first: Romans 9 is also about nations. Paul's examples are Jacob and Esau, of whom God said "two nations are in your womb," Edom in Malachi, and Israel and the Gentiles throughout. If Jeremiah 18 cannot speak to individuals because it concerns nations, the same is true of Romans 9's own examples. And Paul applies Jeremiah's logic to individuals when he says branches will be grafted in again "if they do not continue in unbelief" (11:23).

To the second: agreed. God is the potter and is free. The question is how He uses His freedom, and Jeremiah 18 is God's own answer: He uses it in response to the clay.

To the third: the Orthodox agree that repentance is a gift of grace. St Gregory says "even to wish well needs help from God." But Scripture also shows that grace can be refused: "You always resist the Holy Spirit" (Acts 7:51); "I wanted to gather your children... but you were not willing" (Matthew 23:37). In Jeremiah 18 itself the potter calls, "Return now," and the clay says, "We will walk according to our own plans." If the potter's call were irresistible, that answer could not be given.

To the fourth: Paul does silence the proud objector, as Chrysostom says. But he then gives an answer (9:22–24), and the answer is about God's longsuffering and mercy. And at the end of the chapter he gives the reason Israel stumbled, which is a human reason (9:32).

The Reformed doctrine of unconditional election and reprobation is not "the Protestant view." Protestants have argued about it since the beginning, and two major Protestant traditions reject important parts of it.

The Arminians and Methodists

In 1610 the Dutch followers of Jacobus Arminius set out five articles that the Synod of Dort later rejected. On grace, they said:

The Remonstrance (1610)
Remonstrance, Article IV
"That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good, even to this extent, that the regenerate man himself, without prevenient or assisting, awakening, following and cooperative grace, can neither think, will, nor do good, nor withstand any temptations to evil; so that all good deeds or movements, that can be conceived, must be ascribed to the grace of God in Christ. But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Ghost."
Arminian Articles (1610)VERBATIM · REMONSTRANCE, SCHAFF

John Wesley and the Methodist movement took the same side, and today most Pentecostal, Methodist, Wesleyan and many Baptist Christians reject unconditional reprobation. The Orthodox did not arrive at their view by joining one side of a Protestant argument; they held it a thousand years before that argument began. But it is worth knowing that on Romans 9 the Orthodox stand much closer to most of the world's Protestants than to Geneva and Dort.

An honest page should say where its own argument is strong and where it needs care.

Where it is strong. Jeremiah 18 is not a stray verse. It is the Old Testament's fullest use of the potter image, and God Himself interprets it there. It shows that "the potter has power over the clay" and "the potter responds to the clay" are not opposites in Scripture's own mind. Paul's other vessel passage (2 Timothy 2:21) says the same, and the Fathers read Romans 9 through it. Romans 9:22 (longsuffering), 9:25–26 (not My people become My people), 9:32 (they did not seek it by faith) and 11:23 (if they do not continue in unbelief) all fit Jeremiah's picture and fit poorly with a fixed decree.

Where it needs care. First, Jeremiah 18 does speak of nations, and a Calvinist can say God's conditional dealings in history sit on top of a deeper, unconditional decree. The best answer to that is not Jeremiah alone but the character of God revealed everywhere: He "desires all men to be saved" (1 Timothy 2:4), is "not willing that any should perish" (2 Peter 3:9), and has "no pleasure... that the wicked should die" (Ezekiel 18:23). Scripture does speak of "secret things" that belong to God (Deuteronomy 29:29), but it never presents a hidden will of God that contradicts His revealed desire for the salvation of all. Second, Chrysostom himself warns that Paul uses the potter image for one purpose, to silence presumptuous questioning, and that we should not press every detail of it. That warning applies to everyone. It is a reason not to build a doctrine of reprobation on the image, and also a reason to let God's own explanation of the image in Jeremiah guide how we read it.

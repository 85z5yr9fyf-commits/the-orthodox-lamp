Understanding the Nicene Creed

"...the faith which was once for all delivered to the saints." Jude 3

✦ ✦ ✦
Home›Oriental Orthodoxy›The Nicene Creed
St Anthony's MonasteryCoptic Orthodox · Eastern Desert

At every Divine Liturgy in the Coptic, Armenian, Syriac, Ethiopian, Eritrean and Indian Churches, the faithful stand and say the Creed together. Eastern Orthodox and Roman Catholic Christians say it too, and so do many Lutherans, Anglicans and Presbyterians. It is the one confession of faith that most of the world's Christians share.

That shared confession is a real gift, and it raises a real question. The Creed's last paragraph confesses "one, holy, Catholic and Apostolic Church" and "one Baptism for the remission of sins." Christians who say these words together do not always mean the same thing by them. The Creed was written by bishops gathered in council, taught to candidates for baptism, and guarded by canons. This page reads each article in the sense its authors gave it, from their own catechesis, letters and canons.

The page also takes up a question that comes up often between Orthodox and Protestant Christians: do the Reformation churches hold the Nicene Creed? Their confessions say they do, and receive it explicitly. The Orthodox ask whether the articles on the Church and baptism are received in the sense in which they were written. Section XIII sets out both answers from the primary documents, in each tradition's own words, so that readers can weigh the evidence for themselves.

"...contend earnestly for the faith which was once for all delivered to the saints." Jude 3

A Note on Quotations

Every quotation marked VERBATIM has been checked word for word against a public domain translation: the Ante-Nicene and Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers for the Fathers and councils, Philip Schaff's Creeds of Christendom for the Reformation confessions, and Henry Beveridge's translation of Calvin. The Coptic text of the Creed is quoted as published by the Coptic Orthodox Diocese of the Midlands in the United Kingdom. Scripture follows the NKJV. Where a teaching is summarised rather than quoted, the card is marked PARAPHRASE.

Symbol of Faith
σύμβολον: "token, mark of recognition"
The ancient name for a creed. A symbolon was a token matched against its other half to prove identity; the Creed is the sign by which the faithful recognise one another in the one faith.
Homoousios
ὁμοούσιος: "of one essence"
The word of Nicaea: the Son is of the same essence as the Father, not merely like Him. It is not found in Scripture; the council adopted it to state Scripture's meaning against Arius.
Monogenes
μονογενής: "only-begotten"
The Son's unique and eternal birth from the Father, which sets Him apart from everything that was made (John 1:14, 18).
Ekklesia
ἐκκλησία: "assembly, those called out"
The Church. Cyril of Jerusalem says the Church is rightly given this name "because it calls forth and assembles together all men."
Catholic
καθολικός, from καθ᾽ ὅλου: "according to the whole"
Whole and universal: the Church that holds the whole faith in every place. First used of the Church by St. Ignatius of Antioch, early in the second century.
Apostolic
ἀποστολικός: "of the apostles"
Belonging to the apostles: the faith they preached, the worship they handed on, and the ministry they appointed.
Baptism
βάπτισμα: "immersion, washing"
The washing in water in the name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, by which a person is born again and received into the Church.
Remission
ἄφεσις: "release, forgiveness"
The Creed's "for the remission of sins," εἰς ἄφεσιν ἁμαρτιῶν, is taken from St. Peter's preaching at Pentecost (Acts 2:38).
Anathema
ἀνάθεμα
A solemn exclusion from the communion of the Church. The Creed of 325 closes with an anathema against the teaching of Arius.
Filioque
Latin: "and the Son"
A phrase later added in the West to the article on the Holy Spirit. It is not in the text of 381, nor in the Creed as the Oriental Orthodox pray it.
Rule of Faith
regula fidei
The summary of apostolic teaching handed down in the churches before Nicaea, of which the Creed is the conciliar form.

Three forms of the Creed matter for this page: the Creed issued at Nicaea in 325, the fuller Creed associated with the Council of Constantinople in 381, and the Creed as the Coptic Church prays it today.

The First Ecumenical Council
Creed of Nicaea (AD 325)
"We believe in one God, the Father Almighty, maker of all things visible and invisible; and in one Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of God, the only-begotten of his Father, of the substance of the Father, God of God, Light of Light, very God of very God, begotten (γεννηθέντα), not made, being of one substance (ὁμοούσιον, consubstantialem) with the Father. By whom all things were made, both which be in heaven and in earth. Who for us men and for our salvation came down [from heaven] and was incarnate and was made man. He suffered and the third day he rose again, and ascended into heaven. And he shall come again to judge both the quick and the dead. And [we believe] in the Holy Ghost. ... all that so say, the Catholic and Apostolic Church anathematizes them."
Ecumenical CouncilVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Notice how the Creed of 325 ends. Its anathema against Arius is pronounced by "the Catholic and Apostolic Church." Before the Creed confessed the Church as an article of faith, the Church was already the one speaking it.

The Second Ecumenical Council
Creed of Constantinople (AD 381)
"We believe in one God, the Father Almighty, maker of heaven and earth and of all things visible and invisible. And in one Lord Jesus Christ, the only begotten Son of God, begotten of his Father before all worlds, Light of Light, very God of very God, begotten not made, being of one substance with the Father, by whom all things were made. Who for us men and for our salvation came down from heaven and was incarnate by the Holy Ghost and the Virgin Mary, and was made man, and was crucified also for us under Pontius Pilate. He suffered and was buried, and the third day he rose again according to the Scriptures, and ascended into heaven, and sits at the Right Hand of the Father. And he shall come again with glory to judge both the quick and the dead. Whose kingdom shall have no end. And [we believe] in the Holy Ghost, the Lord and Giver-of-Life, who proceeds from the Father, who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified, who spoke by the prophets. And [we believe] in one, holy, Catholic and Apostolic Church. We acknowledge one Baptism for the remission of sins, [and] we look for the resurrection of the dead and the life of the world to come. Amen."
Ecumenical CouncilVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

The Council of 381 expanded the article on the Holy Spirit against those who denied His divinity, and completed the Creed with the articles on the Church, baptism, the resurrection and the life to come. Its earliest surviving text comes from the acts of the Council of Chalcedon in 451, where it was read alongside the Creed of 325.

Three things stand out. The Coptic text says "we believe," as the Greek of the councils does. It confesses that the Spirit "proceeds from the Father," with nothing added. And it is preceded by a hymn to the Theotokos, which Coptic tradition associates with the Council of Ephesus in 431, the council that upheld her title as Mother of God. Before the Church confesses her faith in the Son, she honours the one from whom He took flesh.

The Creed did not appear from nowhere in 325. It gives conciliar form to a rule of faith the churches had confessed at baptism since the apostles, and its later history explains why Christians who recite it today do not all recite the same text.

c. AD 180
St. Irenaeus records the rule of faith the Church holds throughout the world: one God the Father, one Christ Jesus, and the Holy Spirit who spoke through the prophets.
AD 325
The Council of Nicaea condemns Arius and issues its Creed, confessing the Son "of one substance with the Father." St. Athanasius, then a deacon of Alexandria, attends with his bishop, St. Alexander.
c. AD 350
St. Cyril of Jerusalem delivers his Catechetical Lectures, explaining the creed of his Church article by article to candidates for baptism.
AD 381
The Council of Constantinople confirms the faith of Nicaea, expands the article on the Holy Spirit, and completes the Creed with the articles on the Church, baptism and the life to come.
AD 431
The Council of Ephesus upholds the title Theotokos and forbids anyone to compose "a different Faith as a rival" to that of Nicaea (canon 7).
AD 451
The Creed of 381 is read at Chalcedon, the earliest surviving record of its text. The Oriental Orthodox reject Chalcedon's definition, but they have always kept the Creed itself.
AD 589
In Spain, the Third Council of Toledo is commonly cited as the first council to use the Creed with "and the Son" (filioque) added.
c. 1014
The Creed with the filioque is sung at the Mass in Rome.
1530
The Augsburg Confession, the founding Lutheran confession, opens by affirming "the decree of the Nicene Synod."
1561
The Belgic Confession of the Reformed churches receives "the three creeds" of the Apostles, of Nicaea and of Athanasius.
1571
The Thirty-Nine Articles of the Church of England, in their final English form, declare that the three Creeds are to be received and believed.
1580
The Lutheran Book of Concord is published, beginning with the three ecumenical creeds.
1646
The Westminster Confession of Faith defines the catholic Church as invisible, "the whole number of the elect," alongside the visible Church.

The Creed begins where the apostles began: "for us there is one God, the Father." It does not open with an abstract divine essence but with a Person, the Father, the source of the Son and the Spirit. Its three paragraphs follow the three Names in which every Christian is baptized, which is why the Fathers handed it on as the faith of baptism.

1 Corinthians 8:6
"yet for us there is one God, the Father, of whom are all things, and we for Him; and one Lord Jesus Christ, through whom are all things, and through whom we live."
🔵 Shared reading: Paul's confession already has the Creed's shape: one God the Father, and one Lord Jesus Christ through whom all things were made. The Creed takes up both phrases.
Matthew 28:19
"Go therefore and make disciples of all the nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit,"
🔵 Shared reading: The baptismal command gives the Creed its order. The candidate confesses the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, and is baptized into the one Name of all three.
St. Irenaeus of Lyons
Against Heresies I.10.1 (c. AD 180)
"The Church, though dispersed throughout the whole world, even to the ends of the earth, has received from the apostles and their disciples this faith: [She believes] in one God, the Father Almighty, Maker of heaven, and earth, and the sea, and all things that are in them; and in one Christ Jesus, the Son of God, who became incarnate for our salvation; and in the Holy Spirit, who proclaimed through the prophets the dispensations of God, and the advents, and the birth from a virgin, and the passion, and the resurrection from the dead, and the ascension into heaven in the flesh of the beloved Christ Jesus, our Lord..."
Church FatherVERBATIM · ANF TRANSLATION

Irenaeus wrote about a century and a half before Nicaea, and the outline is already the Creed's. He also says where this faith is found: the Church "has received from the apostles and their disciples this faith." The Creed did not invent the apostolic faith. It gave the Church's long-held confession a fixed conciliar form.

St. Cyril of Jerusalem
Catechetical Lecture 5.12 (c. AD 350)
"...at the proper season expect the confirmation out of Holy Scripture of each part of the contents. For the articles of the Faith were not composed as seemed good to men; but the most important points collected out of all the Scripture make up one complete teaching of the Faith. ... Take heed then, brethren, and hold fast the traditions which you now receive, and write them on the table of your heart."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Cyril holds together two things that are often set against each other. The Creed is drawn "out of all the Scripture," and each article is to be confirmed from Scripture. Yet he hands it to his candidates as "the traditions which you now receive," to be learned by heart before they have been taught its proofs. Scripture and tradition do not compete here. The Church hands on, in a few words, what Scripture teaches at length.

The heart of the Creed of 325 is its confession of the Son: "of the substance of the Father, God of God, Light of Light, very God of very God, begotten, not made," and "of one substance with the Father." Arius taught that the Son was the first and greatest of creatures. The council answered with a word that is not found in Scripture: homoousios, of one essence.

St. Athanasius the Apostolic
De Decretis (Defence of the Nicene Definition) 20 (AD 350s)
"But the Bishops discerning in this too their dissimulation ... were again compelled on their part to collect the sense of the Scriptures, and to re-say and re-write what they had said before, more distinctly still, namely, that the Son is 'one in essence' with the Father"
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

This is the method of Nicaea in the words of the man who defended it for half a century. The bishops did not set out to add to Scripture. They gathered "the sense of the Scriptures" into a word the Arians could not twist. Here Orthodox and Protestant Christians stand on the same ground: both confess the homoousios, and both accept that a council could rightly use a word from outside Scripture to guard Scripture's meaning. The difference, which this page returns to in Sections XIII and XIV, is over what gives such a council its authority.

John 1:1-3
"In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. He was in the beginning with God. All things were made through Him, and without Him nothing was made that was made."
🔵 Shared reading: "By whom all things were made" is John's own phrase. The one through whom everything was made cannot Himself be among the things that were made, which is the whole case against Arius in a sentence.

For the full scriptural and patristic case, see the pages on the Divinity of Christ and the Holy Trinity.

"Who for us men and for our salvation came down from heaven and was incarnate by the Holy Ghost and the Virgin Mary, and was made man." The subject of every verb in this paragraph is the one Lord confessed in the paragraph before. The eternal Son is the one who came down, was incarnate, was crucified, suffered, was buried and rose. The Creed does not divide its story between God and a man. That single subject is the foundation of St. Cyril of Alexandria's Christology and of the Oriental Orthodox confession of one incarnate nature of God the Word; see Miaphysite Christology and the Will of Christ.

Because the one born of the Virgin is the eternal Son, she is rightly called Theotokos, the one who gave birth to God. The Council of Ephesus defended that title in 431, and the Coptic introduction to the Creed confesses it each time the Creed is said: "We glorify you, O holy Virgin, Mother of God, for you have borne for us the Saviour of the whole world." See The Virgin Mary as Theotokos.

"For us men and for our salvation" governs everything that follows. The Creed confesses the incarnation, the cross and the resurrection as acts done for us. Its last paragraph then names the means by which that salvation reaches us: the Holy Spirit, the Church and baptism. The Creed's own order already suggests that these last articles are not optional extras. They answer the question the middle paragraph raises, which is how what Christ did for us becomes ours.

The Creed of 325 ended its third paragraph with a single line: "And [we believe] in the Holy Ghost." By 381 a party had arisen that denied the Spirit's divinity, and the council answered by confessing Him "the Lord and Giver-of-Life, who proceeds from the Father, who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified." He is not called "God" in so many words, but He is given the worship that belongs to God alone.

John 15:26
"But when the Helper comes, whom I shall send to you from the Father, the Spirit of truth who proceeds from the Father, He will testify of Me."
🔵 Orthodox reading: The Creed takes its words from Christ's: the Spirit "proceeds from the Father." The Son sends the Spirit into the world; the Spirit's eternal origin is the Father.
🟣 Protestant reading: Most Protestant churches inherited the Western form of the Creed, which adds "and the Son," together with the Western teaching that the Spirit proceeds eternally from the Father and the Son.
The Third Ecumenical Council
Council of Ephesus, Canon 7 (AD 431)
"...it is unlawful for any man to bring forward, or to write, or to compose a different (ἑτέραν) Faith as a rival to that established by the holy Fathers assembled with the Holy Ghost in Nicæa."
Ecumenical CouncilVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

The Oriental Orthodox receive three ecumenical councils: Nicaea, Constantinople and Ephesus. The Creed they confess is therefore the Creed of 381 as Ephesus guarded it, without later additions. The question of the filioque is treated in full on the Filioque page.

"And [we believe] in one, holy, Catholic and Apostolic Church." The Creed places the Church inside the confession of faith, directly after the Holy Spirit. For the Fathers, the Church is the work of the Spirit, confessed in the same breath as the Spirit Himself. Each of the four words has a history, and the Fathers explain each one.

One

Ephesians 4:4-6
"There is one body and one Spirit, just as you were called in one hope of your calling; one Lord, one faith, one baptism; one God and Father of all, who is above all, and through all, and in you all."
🔵 Orthodox reading: One body stands in the same list as one faith and one baptism. As the faith and the baptism are definite and recognisable, so is the body.
🟣 Protestant reading: The unity of the body is spiritual, shared by all true believers across communions, and it is expressed wherever the Gospel is rightly preached and the sacraments rightly administered.
St. Cyprian of Carthage
On the Unity of the Church 6 (AD 251)
"He can no longer have God for his Father, who has not the Church for his mother. If any one could escape who was outside the Ark of Noah, then he also may escape who shall be outside of the Church."
Church FatherVERBATIM · ANF TRANSLATION

Cyprian, bishop and martyr, wrote this during a schism in Carthage. His concern is division: no one can claim Christ while separating from His body. The image of the Church as mother has a long life, and it appears again, in Calvin, in Section XIII.

Holy

The Church is holy because her Head is holy and the Spirit sanctifies her, not because her members are without sin. Cyril of Jerusalem says that she "universally treats and heals the whole class of sins." Her holiness lies in what she gives, the life of the Spirit in the mysteries, rather than in the record of those who receive it.

Catholic

St. Ignatius of Antioch
Letter to the Smyrnaeans 8 (c. AD 107)
"Let no man do anything connected with the Church without the bishop. Let that be deemed a proper Eucharist, which is [administered] either by the bishop, or by one to whom he has entrusted it. Wherever the bishop shall appear, there let the multitude [of the people] also be; even as, wherever Jesus Christ is, there is the Catholic Church. It is not lawful without the bishop either to baptize or to celebrate a love-feast"
Church FatherVERBATIM · ANF TRANSLATION

This is the earliest surviving use of "catholic" for the Church, written by a bishop on his way to martyrdom in Rome. Notice where Ignatius places the word. The Catholic Church is where Jesus Christ is, and in the local church it is found gathered around the bishop, at a Eucharist the bishop has authorised, and at a baptism he has sanctioned. For Ignatius, catholicity is more than geography: it is the whole Christ present in each church gathered around its bishop.

St. Cyril of Jerusalem
Catechetical Lecture 18.23 (c. AD 350)
"It is called Catholic then because it extends over all the world, from one end of the earth to the other; and because it teaches universally and completely one and all the doctrines which ought to come to men's knowledge, concerning things both visible and invisible, heavenly and earthly; and because it brings into subjection to godliness the whole race of mankind, governors and governed, learned and unlearned; and because it universally treats and heals the whole class of sins, which are committed by soul or body, and possesses in itself every form of virtue which is named, both in deeds and words, and in every kind of spiritual gifts."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Cyril's first reason is geographical: the Church "extends over all the world." Protestant writers rightly point to this, and the Orthodox agree. But Cyril gives four more reasons, and they concern wholeness: the Church teaches the whole faith, gathers every kind of person, heals every kind of sin, and holds every grace. Catholic means universal in reach and complete in content.

St. Cyril of Jerusalem
Catechetical Lecture 18.26 (c. AD 350)
"...that you may avoid their wretched meetings, and ever abide with the Holy Church Catholic in which you were regenerated. And if ever you are sojourning in cities, inquire not simply where the Lord's House is (for the other sects of the profane also attempt to call their own dens houses of the Lord), nor merely where the Church is, but where is the Catholic Church. For this is the peculiar name of this Holy Church, the mother of us all, which is the spouse of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Only-begotten Son of God..."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

This passage matters more than any other for what the Creed meant to those who first confessed it. Cyril's candidates were told that "Catholic" was a name they could ask for in a strange city and expect to find: a visible communion, distinct from the sects that also called their meetings churches. And it is the Church "in which you were regenerated," that is, in which they were baptized. In Cyril's teaching, the Church of the Creed and the font of the Creed belong together.

1 Timothy 3:15
"...the house of God, which is the church of the living God, the pillar and ground of the truth."
🔵 Orthodox reading: Cyril cites this verse for the Catholic Church. The truth is upheld by the Church, which is why the Church could confess it in council and hand it on in the Creed.
🟣 Protestant reading: The Church upholds the truth by faithfully proclaiming Scripture. Her authority is ministerial, exercised under the Word rather than over it.

Apostolic

St. Clement of Rome
First Letter to the Corinthians 42 (c. AD 96)
"The apostles have preached the gospel to us from the Lord Jesus Christ; Jesus Christ [has done so] from God. Christ therefore was sent forth by God, and the apostles by Christ. ... And thus preaching through countries and cities, they appointed the first fruits [of their labours], having first proved them by the Spirit, to be bishops and deacons of those who should afterwards believe."
Church FatherVERBATIM · ANF TRANSLATION
St. Clement of Rome
First Letter to the Corinthians 44 (c. AD 96)
"Our apostles also knew, through our Lord Jesus Christ, that there would be strife on account of the office of the episcopate. For this reason, therefore, inasmuch as they had obtained a perfect fore-knowledge of this, they appointed those [ministers] already mentioned, and afterwards gave instructions, that when these should fall asleep, other approved men should succeed them in their ministry."
Church FatherVERBATIM · ANF TRANSLATION

Clement wrote at the end of the first century, within living memory of the apostles. He describes a chain: God sent Christ, Christ sent the apostles, the apostles appointed bishops and deacons, and they gave instructions that "other approved men should succeed them in their ministry."

St. Irenaeus of Lyons
Against Heresies III.3.1 (c. AD 180)
"It is within the power of all, therefore, in every Church, who may wish to see the truth, to contemplate clearly the tradition of the apostles manifested throughout the whole world; and we are in a position to reckon up those who were by the apostles instituted bishops in the Churches, and [to demonstrate] the succession of these men to our own times"
Church FatherVERBATIM · ANF TRANSLATION
Tertullian of Carthage
The Prescription Against Heretics 32 (c. AD 200)
"Let them produce the original records of their churches; let them unfold the roll of their bishops, running down in due succession from the beginning in such a manner that [that first] bishop shall be able to show for his ordainer and predecessor some one of the apostles or of apostolic men, a man, moreover, who continued steadfast with the apostles."
Early WitnessVERBATIM · ANF TRANSLATION

Irenaeus and Tertullian use the same test against the Gnostics, who claimed a secret tradition from the apostles. Apostolic teaching, they answer, is public, and it can be traced through the bishops of the churches the apostles founded. Neither writer treats succession as a substitute for apostolic doctrine. Succession is how the churches show that their doctrine is the apostles' own. Tertullian later joined the Montanist movement, but on this point he speaks for the Church of his day.

Ephesians 2:19-20
"...members of the household of God, having been built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Jesus Christ Himself being the chief corner stone,"
🔵 Orthodox reading: The apostles are the Church's foundation: the faith they preached, the worship they handed on, and the ministry they appointed to keep both.
🟣 Protestant reading: The foundation is the apostles' teaching, now held in the New Testament. A church is apostolic when it teaches what the apostles taught.
Acts 2:42
"And they continued steadfastly in the apostles' doctrine and fellowship, in the breaking of bread, and in prayers."
🔵 Orthodox reading: The first church's life is described in four parts that cannot be separated: the apostles' doctrine, their fellowship or communion, the Eucharist, and the prayers.
🟣 Protestant reading: These are the marks of a true church in any age: apostolic teaching, fellowship, the Lord's Supper and prayer.

For the full treatment, see Order of the Church & Apostolic Succession and The Authority of the Church.

A creed can be read in isolation, but it was not written in isolation. The bishops who issued the Creed of 325 issued twenty canons at the same council, and the bishops of 381 added canons of their own. If we want to know what these men meant by "the Catholic and Apostolic Church," the plainest evidence is how they governed it.

First Council of Nicaea
Canon 4 (AD 325)
"It is by all means proper that a bishop should be appointed by all the bishops in the province; but should this be difficult, either on account of urgent necessity or because of distance, three at least should meet together, and the suffrages of the absent [bishops] also being given and communicated in writing, then the ordination should take place. But in every province the ratification of what is done should be left to the Metropolitan."

"Concerning those who call themselves Cathari, if they come over to the Catholic and Apostolic Church, the great and holy Synod decrees that they who are ordained shall continue as they are in the clergy. But it is before all things necessary that they should profess in writing that they will observe and follow the dogmas of the Catholic and Apostolic Church..."
Ecumenical CouncilVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

What the Canons Show

Read together, the canons describe the Church the Creed confesses. It has bishops, ordained by other bishops and confirmed by the metropolitan of the province. It has defined territories, so that the Bishop of Alexandria administers Egypt and bishops do not cross into one another's churches. It has presbyters who "offer," and a Eucharist the council calls "the Body of Christ." And it has boundaries: those outside it are received back on stated terms, some by written profession and some by baptism.

None of this settles by itself which body today is the Church of the Creed. It does show what the words meant to the men who wrote them. "The Catholic and Apostolic Church" of 325 and 381 was not an invisible fellowship known only to God. It was a communion of churches with bishops, sacraments and canons, which could receive a schismatic, depose a bishop and anathematise a heresy.

"We acknowledge one Baptism for the remission of sins." The Greek verb is ὁμολογοῦμεν, "we confess." Baptism is the only sacrament named in the Creed, and it is named as an object of faith: the Church does not only practise baptism, she confesses what it does. The phrase "for the remission of sins" is taken from St. Peter's preaching at Pentecost.

The Scriptural Ground

Acts 2:38
"Then Peter said to them, 'Repent, and let every one of you be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins; and you shall receive the gift of the Holy Spirit.'"
🔵 Orthodox reading: Peter joins repentance, baptism and forgiveness in a single command, and the Creed repeats his phrase. Forgiveness is given in baptism and received by repentance and faith.
🟣 Protestant reading: Reformed interpreters read baptism here as the sign and seal of the forgiveness promised to faith. Some Baptist interpreters read "for" in the sense of "because of" a forgiveness already received. Lutherans read the verse much as the Orthodox do.
Acts 22:16
"And now why are you waiting? Arise and be baptized, and wash away your sins, calling on the name of the Lord."
🔵 Orthodox reading: Paul had already met the risen Christ and spent three days in prayer and fasting, yet Ananias tells him to be baptized and "wash away your sins." The washing happens at the font.
🟣 Protestant reading: Many read "calling on the name of the Lord" as the act of faith that saves, with baptism as its outward expression.
John 3:5
"Jesus answered, 'Most assuredly, I say to you, unless one is born of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter the kingdom of God.'"
🔵 Orthodox reading: The Fathers read "born of water and the Spirit" as baptism with near unanimity. Justin and Cyril of Jerusalem both cite this verse for it.
🟣 Protestant reading: Lutherans and many Anglicans read it of baptism. Many Reformed and Baptist interpreters read the water as a figure for the Spirit's cleansing, or for natural birth.
Titus 3:5
"not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to His mercy He saved us, through the washing of regeneration and renewing of the Holy Spirit,"
🔵 Orthodox reading: "The washing of regeneration" is baptism, set in the same sentence as "not by works." Baptism is God's mercy at work, not a human work that competes with grace.
🟣 Protestant reading: The Lutheran reading agrees. Others read the washing as the inner cleansing of the Spirit, which outward baptism signifies.
1 Peter 3:21
"There is also an antitype which now saves us, baptism (not the removal of the filth of the flesh, but the answer of a good conscience toward God), through the resurrection of Jesus Christ,"
🔵 Orthodox reading: "Baptism... now saves us," and its power comes from the resurrection of Christ. The water does not save as water; Christ saves in the water.
🟣 Protestant reading: The weight falls on the parenthesis: what saves is not the outward washing but "the answer of a good conscience toward God."
Romans 6:3-4
"Or do you not know that as many of us as were baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into His death? Therefore we were buried with Him through baptism into death, that just as Christ was raised from the dead by the glory of the Father, even so we also should walk in newness of life."
🔵 Orthodox reading: Baptism unites the believer to Christ's death and resurrection. It is the beginning of the new life, not only a picture of it.
🟣 Protestant reading: All agree that the passage speaks of union with Christ. Traditions differ over whether baptism brings about that union or signifies and seals it.

The Fathers on Baptism

St. Justin Martyr
First Apology 61 (c. AD 155)
"Then they are brought by us where there is water, and are regenerated in the same manner in which we were ourselves regenerated. For, in the name of God, the Father and Lord of the universe, and of our Saviour Jesus Christ, and of the Holy Spirit, they then receive the washing with water. ... in order that we may not remain the children of necessity and of ignorance, but may become the children of choice and knowledge, and may obtain in the water the remission of sins formerly committed..."
Church FatherVERBATIM · ANF TRANSLATION

Justin is describing the practice of the Church at Rome to a pagan emperor, a century and a half before Nicaea. Baptism is regeneration; it is in the Name of the Trinity; and in the water the candidate obtains "the remission of sins formerly committed." The Creed's phrase was already the Church's plain description of what happens at the font.

St. Cyril of Jerusalem
Catechetical Lecture 3.3–4 (c. AD 350)
"Regard not the Laver as simple water, but rather regard the spiritual grace that is given with the water. ... When going down, therefore, into the water, think not of the bare element, but look for salvation by the power of the Holy Ghost: for without both you can not possibly be made perfect."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION
St. Cyril of Jerusalem
Catechetical Lecture 3.10 (c. AD 350)
"If any man receive not Baptism, he has not salvation; except only Martyrs, who even without the water receive the kingdom. For when the Saviour, in redeeming the world by His Cross, was pierced in the side, He shed forth blood and water; that men, living in times of peace, might be baptized in water, and, in times of persecution, in their own blood."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Cyril's exception is worth noticing. Martyrs who die for Christ before reaching the font are baptized in their own blood. The water is what God has given to His Church, and Cyril urges it without hesitation; yet he does not imagine God unable to save the martyr whom persecution kept from it.

St. Basil the Great
On the Holy Spirit 12.28 (c. AD 375)
"Faith and baptism are two kindred and inseparable ways of salvation: faith is perfected through baptism, baptism is established through faith, and both are completed by the same names. For as we believe in the Father and the Son and the Holy Ghost, so are we also baptized in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Ghost; first comes the confession, introducing us to salvation, and baptism follows, setting the seal upon our assent."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Basil states the relation that later debates pulled apart. Faith and baptism are not rivals. Faith is "perfected through baptism," and baptism is "established through faith." The confession comes first and the font seals it, which is the order of the Creed itself, learned and confessed by candidates before their baptism.

St. Gregory the Theologian
Oration 40, On Holy Baptism, 3 (AD 381)
"Illumination is the splendour of souls, the conversion of the life, the question put to the Godward conscience. It is the aid to our weakness, the renunciation of the flesh, the following of the Spirit, the fellowship of the Word, the improvement of the creature, the overwhelming of sin, the participation of light, the dissolution of darkness."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Why "One" Baptism

"One" means unrepeatable. As there is "one Lord, one faith, one baptism," a person is born again only once. Nicaea's canons show how carefully the Church applied this. The Cathari, schismatics whose baptism was the Church's own, were received without being baptized again, and their clergy kept their rank (canon 8). The Paulianists, followers of Paul of Samosata, whose teaching on God and on Christ was heretical, "must by all means be rebaptized" (canon 19), because what they had received was not the one baptism at all.

For how the Oriental Orthodox apply this today, see The Acceptance of External Baptism, and for the baptism of children, Infant vs Believers' Baptism.

"We look for the resurrection of the dead and the life of the world to come." The Creed ends in hope. The Coptic text reads "the life of the age to come," which is closer to the Greek αἰῶνος, "age."

St. Cyril of Jerusalem
Catechetical Lecture 18.28 (c. AD 350)
"In this Holy Catholic Church receiving instruction and behaving ourselves virtuously, we shall attain the kingdom of heaven, and inherit eternal life; for which also we endure all toils, that we may be made partakers thereof from the Lord."
Church FatherVERBATIM · NPNF TRANSLATION

Cyril joins the last articles in a single sentence. It is in the Holy Catholic Church, instructed and living virtuously, that Christians reach the kingdom. The order of the Creed's last paragraph is the order of salvation: the Spirit gives life, the Church receives it, baptism begins it, and the resurrection completes it.

This question is often asked with heat. It deserves to be answered with documents. The magisterial Reformation (Lutheran, Reformed and Anglican) did not reject the Creed. It received it formally, and those churches confess it to this day. What follows sets out, in the Reformers' own words, what they affirmed and how they read the articles on the Church and baptism.

What the Reformation Churches Affirm

The Lutheran Churches
Augsburg Confession, Article I (1530)
"The churches, with common consent among us, do teach that the decree of the Nicene Synod concerning the unity of the divine essence and of the three persons is true, and without doubt to be believed: to wit, that there is one divine essence which is called and is God, eternal, without body, indivisible [without part], of infinite power, wisdom, goodness, the Creator and Preserver of all things, visible and invisible; and that yet there are three persons of the same essence and power, who also are co-eternal, the Father, the Son, and the Holy Ghost."
Lutheran ConfessionVERBATIM · SCHAFF, CREEDS OF CHRISTENDOM
The Reformed Churches
Belgic Confession, Article IX (1561)
"Therefore, in this point, we do willingly receive the three creeds, namely, that of the Apostles, of Nice, and of Athanasius; likewise that which, conformable thereunto, is agreed upon by the ancient fathers."
Reformed ConfessionVERBATIM · SCHAFF, CREEDS OF CHRISTENDOM
The Church of England
Thirty-Nine Articles, Article VIII (1571 text)
"The three Credes, Nicene Crede, Athanasian Crede, and that whiche is commonlye called the Apostles' Crede, ought throughlye to be receaued and beleued: for they may be proued by moste certayne warrauntes of holye scripture."
Anglican ArticlesVERBATIM · SCHAFF, CREEDS OF CHRISTENDOM

The agreement here is real and should be said plainly. On the Trinity and the person of Christ, the confessions of Augsburg, the Netherlands and England stand with Nicaea against Arius, and they condemn the same ancient heresies the Fathers condemned. Notice also the reason the English Articles give: the Creeds are to be believed "for they may be proued by moste certayne warrauntes of holye scripture." The Creed is received because, and in so far as, it agrees with Scripture.

How They Read "One Holy Catholic and Apostolic Church"

The Lutheran Churches
Augsburg Confession, Article VII (1530)
"Also they teach that one holy Church is to continue forever. But the Church is the congregation of saints [the assembly of all believers], in which the Gospel is rightly taught [purely preached] and the Sacraments rightly administered [according to the Gospel]. And unto the true unity of the Church, it is sufficient to agree concerning the doctrine of the Gospel and the administration of the Sacraments."
Lutheran ConfessionVERBATIM · SCHAFF, CREEDS OF CHRISTENDOM
The Reformed Churches
Belgic Confession, Article XXVII (1561)
"We believe and profess one catholic or universal Church, which is a holy congregation and assembly of true Christian believers, expecting all their salvation in Jesus Christ, being washed by his blood, sanctified and sealed by the Holy Ghost."
Reformed ConfessionVERBATIM · SCHAFF, CREEDS OF CHRISTENDOM
The Reformed Churches
Westminster Confession of Faith 25.1–2 (1646)
"The catholic or universal Church, which is invisible, consists of the whole number of the elect, that have been, are, or shall be gathered into one, under Christ the head thereof; and is the spouse, the body, the fullness of him that filleth all in all. ... The visible Church, which is also catholic or universal under the gospel (not confined to one nation as before under the law) consists of all those, throughout the world, that profess the true religion, and of their children; and is the kingdom of the Lord Jesus Christ, the house and family of God, out of which there is no ordinary possibility of salvation."
Reformed ConfessionVERBATIM · SCHAFF, CREEDS OF CHRISTENDOM
The Church of England
Thirty-Nine Articles, Article XIX
"The visible Church of Christ is a congregation of faithful men, in the which the pure Word of God is preached, and the Sacraments be duly ministered according to Christ's ordinance, in all those things that of necessity are requisite to the same."
Anglican ArticlesVERBATIM · SCHAFF, CREEDS OF CHRISTENDOM
John Calvin
Institutes of the Christian Religion IV.1.4 (1559)
"But as it is now our purpose to discourse of the visible Church, let us learn, from her single title of Mother, how useful, nay, how necessary the knowledge of her is, since there is no other means of entering into life unless she conceive us in the womb and give us birth, unless she nourish us at her breasts, and, in short, keep us under her charge and government, until, divested of mortal flesh, we become like the angels ... Moreover, beyond the pale of the Church no forgiveness of sins, no salvation, can be hoped for"
ReformerVERBATIM · BEVERIDGE TRANSLATION (1845)

The Reformers did not abandon the visible Church. Calvin calls her mother in words close to Cyprian's, and Westminster says that outside the visible Church "there is no ordinary possibility of salvation." What changed is how the Church is identified. She is known by her marks, the Gospel rightly preached and the sacraments rightly administered, rather than by communion with bishops in succession from the apostles. Westminster also sets alongside the visible Church "the catholic or universal Church, which is invisible," made up of the elect. That distinction has roots in St. Augustine's teaching on the Church as a mixed body, but the Creed itself does not draw it.

How They Read "One Baptism for the Remission of Sins"

The Lutheran Churches
Augsburg Confession, Article IX (1530)
"Of Baptism they teach that it is necessary to salvation, and that by Baptism the grace of God is offered, and that children are to be baptized, who by Baptism, being offered to God, are received into God's favor."
Lutheran ConfessionVERBATIM · SCHAFF, CREEDS OF CHRISTENDOM
The Church of England
Thirty-Nine Articles, Article XXVII
"Baptism is not only a sign of profession, and mark of difference, whereby Christian men are discerned from others that be not christened, but it is also a sign of Regeneration or New-Birth, whereby, as by an instrument, they that receive Baptism rightly are grafted into the Church; the promises of the forgiveness of sin, and of our adoption to be the sons of God by the Holy Ghost, are visibly signed and sealed..."
Anglican ArticlesVERBATIM · SCHAFF, CREEDS OF CHRISTENDOM
The Reformed Churches
Westminster Confession of Faith 28.5 (1646)
"Although it be a great sin to contemn or neglect this ordinance, yet grace and salvation are not so inseparably annexed unto it, as that no person can be regenerated or saved without it, or that all that are baptized are undoubtedly regenerated."
Reformed ConfessionVERBATIM · SCHAFF, CREEDS OF CHRISTENDOM

On baptism the Reformation churches differ among themselves. The Augsburg Confession teaches that baptism "is necessary to salvation." The Thirty-Nine Articles call it an "instrument" by which those who receive it rightly "are grafted into the Church." The Westminster Confession makes it a sign and seal whose grace is "not so inseparably annexed unto it" that no one can be saved without it. Baptist and many evangelical churches treat baptism as an ordinance of obedience for believers, signifying a salvation already received. Some free-church traditions, notably the Restoration movement with its slogan "no creed but the Bible," do not use creeds at all.

Same Words, Different Meanings?

Both sides of this question can be put in their strongest form.

The Protestant case. The Creed's words are Scripture's words, and Scripture should interpret them. "Catholic" means universal, as Cyril's first definition says, and Protestants confess the universal Church of all believers. The Creed nowhere mentions bishops or succession, and adding them reads later practice into the text. The Reformers did not think they were leaving the ancient Church; they thought they were recovering it, which is why the Augsburg Confession appeals to the Fathers and Calvin borrows Cyprian's language. And on baptism, the Lutheran and Anglican confessions affirm much of what the Fathers taught.

The Orthodox case. A creed means what its confessors meant, and the bishops of 325 and 381 have told us what they meant, in canons written at the same councils. Catechists such as Cyril taught that meaning to the people who first recited the Creed. In that teaching the "Catholic Church" is a visible communion one can find in a city and distinguish from the sects, the Church "in which you were regenerated." The Church that wrote the Creed was episcopal, eucharistic and bounded by canons. And "one baptism for the remission of sins" is Peter's preaching at Pentecost, and the Fathers quoted on this page teach that sins are forgiven in that washing. To confess the words while identifying the Church by different marks, or while treating baptism as a sign of forgiveness already received, is to give the words a meaning their authors did not give them.

Where that leaves the question. No one doubts that Lutheran, Reformed and Anglican Christians confess the Creed sincerely, and on the Trinity and the person of Christ they confess it in the same sense as the Fathers. The disagreement lies in the last paragraph. It is not about whether the words are said, but about whether "Church" and "baptism" are confessed in the sense the councils gave them. The documents above are set out so that readers can make that comparison for themselves.

The council that confessed "the Catholic and Apostolic Church" was an assembly of bishops, and its canons regulated their ordination (Section X). Irenaeus and Tertullian, more than a century earlier, had already identified the apostolic churches as those that could show their bishops' succession from the apostles.

Succession is never offered as a replacement for apostolic doctrine. Irenaeus appeals to it precisely as the guarantee of doctrine against those who claimed secret teaching. The Orthodox hold the two together as he did: the apostolic faith, kept by churches whose ministry descends from the apostles.

A universal Church that cannot be found in a city is not the Church Cyril was describing.

The Apostles' Creed is a Western baptismal creed that grew from the ancient creed of the Church of Rome. The Oriental Orthodox Churches use the Creed of Nicaea and Constantinople in their liturgy and prayers.

The people say it together in the Divine Liturgy, before the Prayer of Reconciliation, and it is prayed in the hours of the Agpeya, preceded by the Introduction to the Creed.

Coptic tradition associates it with the Council of Ephesus in 431, which upheld the title Theotokos. Whatever its exact origin, it has long stood before the Creed in Coptic prayer, so that the Church honours the Mother of God before confessing the incarnation of her Son.

Many do, sincerely, and on the Trinity and the person of Christ they confess what the councils confessed.
